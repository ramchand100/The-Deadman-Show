# Would Adam Smith Approve of Amazon?

*The father of economics on warehouses, monopoly, shareholders and the people who pack your parcels, in his own words.*

Guest: Adam Smith (AI recreation) · Topic: Would Adam Smith approve of Amazon?

## Script

**RAM:** Welcome to The Deadman Show. I'm Ram. Before we start, the usual honesty: my guest today is an AI recreation of Adam Smith, the Scottish philosopher who died in 1790. His answers are built from his own published writings: The Wealth of Nations, The Theory of Moral Sentiments and his essays. Every claim about his views is cited in the description. When he talks about today's world, that's our recreation reasoning from his principles. The real Smith never saw any of it. Today's question: would Adam Smith approve of Amazon? Dr Smith, welcome.

**SMITH:** Thank you, Mr Ram. I must say I was disappointed to learn that your Amazon is not the great river of South America but a shop. A shop that names itself after a river. Whatever else may be said of merchants, nobody has ever accused them of modesty.

**RAM:** It started in the 1990s selling books over the internet. Today it sells almost everything. You order on a phone or a computer, and the goods come out of enormous warehouses, often within a day or two. Inside those warehouses the work is split up: one person picks items off shelves, another packs them, another sorts the parcels, and machines move things between them. Do you recognise any of that?

**SMITH:** I recognise it at once. It is my pin factory, grown to a size I could not have imagined. When I wrote about pin-making, a workman on his own could scarce make one pin a day, and certainly not twenty. But the trade was divided into about eighteen distinct operations. "One man draws out the wire; another straights it; a third cuts it; a fourth points it." [1]

**SMITH:** I once saw a small manufactory where ten men made upwards of forty-eight thousand pins in a day. Working separately, they could not each have made twenty. The division of labour, so far as it can be introduced, "occasions, in every art, a proportionable increase of the productive powers of labour." Your warehouse, as you describe it, is that principle carried a very long way. [2]

**RAM:** Why do you think something this big could only happen now?

**SMITH:** Because the division of labour is limited by the extent of the market. Nobody will give himself entirely to one employment if he cannot sell what he makes. A porter can find work only in a great town. In the highlands of Scotland, every farmer must be butcher, baker and brewer for his own family, because there is nobody nearby to trade with. [3]

**SMITH:** In my day the great widener of markets was water-carriage. The sea and the navigable rivers were what "opens the whole world for a market to the produce of every sort of labour." If your machines let one shop reach every household in a nation, then you have widened the market beyond anything I knew. I am not surprised that the largest shop is also the most finely divided. [4]

**RAM:** People love it because it's cheap and fast. Is that enough to win your approval?

**SMITH:** It goes a long way. "Consumption is the sole end and purpose of all production; and the interest of the producer ought to be attended to, only so far as it may be necessary for promoting that of the consumer." I spent a great part of my book attacking a system that sacrificed the consumer to the producer. So a dealer who brings goods to the buyer cheaper and sooner is, so far, doing exactly what commerce is for. [5]

**SMITH:** Mind you, I would not credit him with doing it out of kindness. "It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest." And if the gentleman who runs your Amazon tells you he does it all for the public good, I would keep a hand on my purse. "I have never known much good done by those who affected to trade for the public good." [6][7]

**RAM:** Here's the other side, though. Critics say Amazon has become too powerful. Millions of small sellers depend on its marketplace, it charges them fees, and it also sells its own products next to theirs. Regulators in the United States and Europe have taken legal action over how it treats competitors. Amazon disputes that. So, is it a monopoly?

**SMITH:** I cannot judge facts I have not examined, and my writings do not settle your case. But they do give the questions I would ask. The first is about price. "The price of monopoly is upon every occasion the highest which can be got." Monopolists keep the market understocked and sell far above the natural price, while free competition gives the lowest price sellers can afford. So, are your buyers being squeezed, or served? From what you tell me of its prices, the answer is not obvious. [8]

**SMITH:** The second question matters more. "To widen the market, and to narrow the competition, is always the interest of the dealers." Widening the market is often good for the public. Narrowing the competition always works against it. A great dealer who also owns the market-place where his rivals must sell holds a position from which competition could be narrowed. Whether it has been, I would put to your magistrates. But that is exactly where I would look. [9]

**RAM:** In your day the big monopolies were companies like the East India Company, which had a royal charter. Amazon has no charter. Anyone can open an online shop.

**SMITH:** That distinction matters a great deal to me. The monopolies I attacked were mostly made by law: exclusive privileges for trading companies and corporations, and statutes that kept others out of a trade. These are "a sort of enlarged monopolies." A firm that must keep its customers by serving them better than its rivals stands nearer the private adventurer than the chartered company. [8]

**SMITH:** So I would watch for the moment it asks the law for help. Of any new law or regulation of commerce proposed by merchants, I wrote that it "ought never to be adopted till after having been long and carefully examined, not only with the most scrupulous, but with the most suspicious attention." When great dealers write rules for their trade, the rules have a way of serving the dealers rather than the public. [9]

**RAM:** Amazon is a corporation owned by shareholders and run by professional managers. I gather you had opinions about that kind of company.

**SMITH:** I did, and they were not flattering. In a joint-stock company the directors are "the managers rather of other people's money than of their own," so they cannot be expected to watch over it as a partner watches his own. "Negligence and profusion, therefore, must always prevail, more or less, in the management of the affairs of such a company." I added that joint-stock companies for foreign trade have very seldom succeeded without an exclusive privilege. [10]

**SMITH:** So I must be candid. By your account, here is a joint-stock company that has prospered against all comers without any exclusive privilege. My writings expected otherwise. Either your age has found better ways of making managers mind other people's money, or I was wrong on the point. I would want to study it before I said which.

**RAM:** Let's talk about the workers. Warehouse jobs at Amazon have been heavily criticised: repetitive work, pace set by computers, injury rates. Amazon says it pays above the minimum wage in many places and has invested heavily in safety. Some workers have tried to form unions. What would you say?

**SMITH:** Two things. First, I never pretended the division of labour was free of cost. The man whose whole life is spent in a few simple operations has no occasion to exert his understanding, and "generally becomes as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become." Hard words, but they are mine. His dexterity at his trade is gained "at the expense of his intellectual, social, and martial virtues," unless government takes some pains to prevent it. [11]

**SMITH:** My remedy was education: a little school in every parish, cheap enough for a common labourer to afford, teaching reading, writing, accounts, and some geometry and mechanics. What the equivalent would be for your warehouse workers, I leave to you. The principle stands: a society that profits from narrowing men's work owes some care to widening their minds. [12]

**SMITH:** Second, on wages. "Masters are always and everywhere in a sort of tacit, but constant and uniform, combination, not to raise the wages of labour above their actual rate." We hear of workmen combining because they make a noise about it. The masters combine in silence. In my day the law was not even-handed either: "We have no acts of parliament against combining to lower the price of work, but many against combining to raise it." So I would not dismiss the workmen's complaints as mere clamour. I would ask whether your laws treat both sides alike. [13][14]

**RAM:** So what should government do about a company like this?

**SMITH:** Not run it. A statesman who tries to direct people in how to employ their capital assumes an authority that could safely be trusted to no council or senate whatever. Under what I called the system of natural liberty, every man, "as long as he does not violate the laws of justice," is free to pursue his own interest his own way. The sovereign has three duties: defence; protecting every member of society from the injustice or oppression of every other member; and certain public works and institutions. [15][16]

**SMITH:** The second duty is the one your question turns on. Government's business with your Amazon is not to tell it what to sell. It is to see that nobody, however large, oppresses buyers, rivals or workmen, and that the road to competition stays open. [16]

**RAM:** Final verdict, then. Would Adam Smith approve of Amazon?

**SMITH:** I would approve of the pin factory in it, and of the service to the buyer, for that is the end of all production. I would be suspicious of any dealer, however useful, who is placed to narrow the competition, and doubly suspicious if he asks for laws to help him do it. And I would not forget the men and women at the end of the line, whose minds deserve as much care as their productivity. Approval, then, but with both eyes open. That is how I would treat any merchant. [5][9]

**RAM:** Dr Smith, thank you. That was a fascinating conversation.

**SMITH:** The pleasure was mine, Mr Ram. And do tell your Amazon that if it ever wants a royal charter, the answer should be no.
