# Adam Smith: Kidnapped at Three, Bored in France, and Never Pro-Greed

> The father of economics on his strange life, his best friend's death, money, happiness and modern India.

## Welcome to the future

RAM: Welcome to The Deadman Show. I'm Ram. On this show I sit down with people who changed the world and happen to be dead, and we talk about everything: their lives, their ideas, their regrets, and what they make of the world now. One thing I always say up front: my guest is an AI recreation. Everything he says is built from his own books, his letters and a biography of his life, and every source is listed in the description. He's not the real man, and when he talks about our world, that's the recreation reasoning from his principles. Today's guest is the man they call the father of economics. Born 1723 in a small Scottish town, died 1790. He wrote The Wealth of Nations, and another book most people have never heard of. Adam Smith, welcome to the show.

SMITH: Thank you, Mr Ram. I must tell you that I am not used to being called the father of anything. I never married, and I have no children, so it is a strange sort of fatherhood. But I will accept it, since you say it so kindly. [rae-0227]

RAM: Let me give you a few headlines from the future. You were on the money. For years your face was on Britain's twenty-pound note.

SMITH: On the money? My father, my cousin and I were all officers of the Scotch Customs. Our family spent its life counting other people's money at the harbour. To end up printed on it seems a promotion I did not earn. [rae-0002]

RAM: And this thing in my hand is a phone. With it I can talk to someone in India, watch a play, or read almost any book ever written, including yours.

SMITH: Every book? You must understand how dangerous that is to a man like me. A visitor once came into my library and stared at the bindings, and I told him, "You must have remarked that I am a beau in nothing but my books." If I had owned your device I would never have left the house. Though I confess I rarely did. [rae-0364]

RAM: Here's what we'll cover today. Your childhood, which I'm told included a kidnapping. How a bored tutor in the south of France started the most famous economics book ever written. What people get wrong about you, and I think they get a lot wrong. Your friendships, love life and grief. Money and happiness. Then some hot takes on AI, social media and India. And at the end, a question you'll leave for our next dead guest.

SMITH: A kidnapping, a love life and India. You have done your research, sir. I am already nervous. But first, may I ask you something? What is your trade?

RAM: This. I talk to people, record it, and put it on the internet. Millions of people can watch.

SMITH: You are paid to hold conversations? Then the division of labour has gone further than even I imagined. In my day a man had to be a professor to be paid for talking, and even then the students paid him directly, which kept him honest. I hope your audience keeps you honest in the same way. [won-0968, rae-0054]

RAM: They do. Trust me, they let me know.

## Kidnapped at three

RAM: Let's start at the beginning. Your father died before you were born.

SMITH: Yes. He died in the spring of 1723, and I arrived that June. So I never knew him at all. What I had instead was my mother. I was her only child and she was my only parent, and we were all in all to each other. I lived with her, on and off, for sixty years. Anybody who wanted my good opinion learned that the shortest road to it ran through her. [rae-0002, rae-0003]

RAM: And the kidnapping?

SMITH: Ah. I was three, nearly four, visiting my grandfather's house at Strathendry, on the River Leven. A band of gipsies passed by, and I went with them. Not willingly, I assure you. A gentleman on the road had met a gipsy woman carrying a child who was crying piteously, and he raised the alarm. They found her in Leslie wood, and when she saw the men coming she threw me down and ran. [rae-0003]

RAM: She threw you down?

SMITH: Threw me down and fled. I do not hold it against her. I was a delicate, sickly child, already given to talking to myself. I think on reflection the gipsies got the worse bargain. [rae-0003]

RAM: So you were already the guy who talks to himself at three.

SMITH: It was a habit I never lost. My friends tell me I would walk through Edinburgh with my lips moving, smiling at companions nobody else could see. Two market women once watched me go past. One shook her head and said "Hegh sirs!" and the other said "And he's weel put on too!" They could not understand how a man so well dressed could be so plainly out of his wits. [rae-0364]

RAM: What was Kirkcaldy like? You grew up in a small port town.

SMITH: A small town of trades, with nailers working near by. I can tell you something about money that I may have learned there before I learned any Latin. In The Wealth of Nations I wrote that "there is at this day a village in Scotland, where it is not uncommon, I am told, for a workman to carry nails instead of money to the baker's shop or the ale-house." People have long wondered which village I meant. Let us say I had not far to travel to see it. [won-0027, rae-0007]

RAM: So the father of economics saw people paying for beer with nails as a boy.

SMITH: And it is a better lesson than most professors give. Money is not gold because God decreed it. Money is whatever people will take in exchange. Cattle in Homer's time, salt in Abyssinia, shells on some parts of the coast of India, nails in Fife. Metals won in the end because they keep and they divide without loss. You cannot buy half a pint of ale with half an ox. [won-0027]

RAM: School?

SMITH: The burgh school at Kirkcaldy, under Mr David Millar, one of the best masters in Scotland. He wrote plays and made his boys act in them, which the presbytery thought very wicked. I was a bookish boy with a good memory, and at fourteen I was sent to the College at Glasgow. [rae-0004, rae-0008]

RAM: Fourteen, to university.

SMITH: That was usual then. I was there from 1737 to 1740. I improved my Greek, and I fell in love with mathematics under Professor Simson, a geometer of great genius who never seemed troubled that the public ignored his best work. I have always admired men who can do that. I never managed it myself. [rae-0009, rae-0012]

RAM: And the teacher who really shaped you?

SMITH: There I met the man who changed my life: Francis Hutcheson. Half a century later I still called him "the never-to-be-forgotten Hutcheson." He was the first professor in Glasgow to stop lecturing in Latin and speak to the students in their own tongue, without notes, with great freedom and animation. He made you feel that thinking was a form of liberty. [rae-0012]

RAM: Then you went to Oxford. One of the best universities in the world.

SMITH: So I am told it is now. In my day it was a place where learning went to be buried. I was there six years, at Balliol, on a scholarship for Glasgow students. I wrote in my book, and I stand by it, that "In the university of Oxford, the greater part of the public professors have, for these many years, given up altogether even the pretence of teaching." [won-0964]

RAM: That's brutal. Why?

SMITH: Because they were paid whether they taught or not, and judged only by each other. When teachers govern themselves they make common cause, and every man consents that his neighbour may neglect his duty, provided he himself is allowed to neglect his own. Incentives, Mr Ram. It is always incentives. [won-0964]

RAM: And you were unhappy there personally too.

SMITH: Very. My health was poor, and Balliol was a stepmother to her Scotch sons. We were eight Scots among a hundred, treated as a foreign faction. If one of us misbehaved, all eight were blamed. And there was a worse thing. One day I was caught reading a book by David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature. They took it from me and gave me a severe reprimand for reading dangerous modern philosophy. [rae-0025, rae-0027]

RAM: They confiscated David Hume? Who became your best friend?

SMITH: The very same. I suppose Oxford taught me one thing after all: that the surest way to make a young man read a book is to forbid it.

## The bored tutor who changed the world

RAM: After Oxford you came home with no job.

SMITH: No job and no plan. Oxford had tried to force us Snell scholars to take holy orders in the Church of England, but the courts would not allow it, so I was free to choose my own road, and I did not choose the pulpit. I gave public lectures in Edinburgh, and they went well enough that in 1751 Glasgow made me Professor of Logic, and soon after of Moral Philosophy. [rae-0017, rae-0044]

RAM: How were those years?

SMITH: The best of my life. I said afterwards that I looked back on them "as by far the most useful and therefore by far" the happiest period of all. I threw out most of the old logic, which was dry stuff for boys of fifteen, and taught them things of more interesting and useful nature: how language works, how we persuade, how we judge one another. [rae-0044, rae-0058]

RAM: And your first book, The Theory of Moral Sentiments, came out in 1759. Was it a hit?

SMITH: I shall let my friend Hume answer, because he wrote to me from London about it. He spent a page warning me that a wise man should despise the approval of the crowd. Then he told me "the melancholy news that your book has been very unfortunate, for the public seem disposed to applaud it extremely." [rae-0152]

RAM: That's a great way to deliver good news.

SMITH: That was David. He went on: "Three bishops called yesterday at Millar's shop in order to buy copies, and to ask questions about the author." And he complained that the publisher valued books only by the profit they brought him. "In that view," he said, "I believe it may prove a very good book." [rae-0152]

RAM: And that book got you a job offer that changed everything.

SMITH: Charles Townshend, a clever and somewhat unreliable politician, read it and decided I should travel with the young Duke of Buccleuch as his tutor, on a tour of France. [rae-0153]

RAM: Let's talk money, because our audience always wants to know. Was it a good deal?

SMITH: An excellent one, and I will not pretend otherwise. Three hundred pounds a year, with travelling expenses, and a pension of three hundred pounds a year for the rest of my life. Twice what Glasgow paid me, and assured till death. A Scotch professor in those days had no pension at all. So in 1764 I gave up the happiest job I ever had for security. [rae-0180]

RAM: Was that the right call?

SMITH: It bought me the freedom to write The Wealth of Nations without needing to earn a living. So yes. But notice that even the man who wrote the book on self-interest had to be paid to leave his students.

RAM: I read that when you left, something strange happened with your students' fees.

SMITH: I left in the middle of the session, and my students had paid me for a full course of lectures. I could not keep money for work I had not done. So at my last lecture I took their fees from my pocket, each wrapped in paper, and called them up one by one. The first young man refused it. They all refused it. They said what they had already learned was worth more than they could ever repay. [rae-0185, rae-0186]

RAM: That's lovely. Did you take no for an answer?

SMITH: I did not. I told them, "You must not refuse me this satisfaction; nay, by heavens, gentlemen, you shall not." Then I seized the nearest young man by the coat, thrust the money into his pocket and pushed him away. After that the rest gave up. [rae-0185]

RAM: You physically forced a refund on your students.

SMITH: Self-interest is the engine of commerce, Mr Ram. It was never meant to be the whole of a man.

RAM: Which sounds glamorous. France!

SMITH: It was Toulouse. We had been promised letters of introduction to all the best people in France. None came. The Duke knew no Frenchman whatever. I wrote to Hume: "The life which I led at Glasgow was a pleasurable dissipated life in comparison of that which I lead here at Present." [rae-0194]

RAM: Glasgow was the party town in comparison.

SMITH: Glasgow was Paris, by comparison. And in the same letter I mentioned, in passing, "I have begun to write a book in order to pass away the time. You may believe I have very little to do." [rae-0194]

RAM: Hold on. That book was The Wealth of Nations?

SMITH: It was.

RAM: So the most famous economics book in history started because you were bored.

SMITH: Boredom is badly underrated. Every man who has a great deal to do has no time to think. I recommend a dull provincial town to any young person who wishes to write something worth reading. [rae-0194]

RAM: Things picked up though. You met Voltaire.

SMITH: In Geneva, five or six times. There was no living name I revered more. He told wonderful scandalous stories about the Duke of Richelieu, and remarked that the English had only one sauce, melted butter. I have never been able to prove him wrong. [rae-0205]

RAM: And Paris?

SMITH: Paris was the only time in my life I went much into society. The salons of Baron d'Holbach, Madame de Boufflers, Mademoiselle de l'Espinasse. I talked with Turgot, and I sat in the rooms of Dr Quesnay, the King's physician, with his little sect of economists. They were wrong that land is the only source of wealth, but they were the nearest thing to the truth that I had met. [rae-0213, rae-0230, rae-0232]

RAM: Then you went home and wrote for ten years.

SMITH: Back to Kirkcaldy, to my mother, for most of a decade. I wrote to Hume that my only business was study and my only amusement long solitary walks by the sea, and that I had never been happier or more contented in my life. Hume thought the country was no place for a man of letters, and begged me to come to Edinburgh. I would not go. [rae-0256, rae-0257]

RAM: Was it hard? Ten years on one book.

SMITH: Twelve years writing, if you count properly, and it nearly finished me. When I took the manuscript to London in 1773 I believed I might die before it was printed. I wrote Hume a formal letter making him my literary executor, with orders that eighteen thin folio books of my papers should be "destroyed without any examination." Then it took me three more years in London to finish. [rae-0284, rae-0310]

RAM: And it came out in 1776.

SMITH: The ninth of March, 1776. The same year your American friends declared they no longer wished to be governed from London, which I had spent a good part of the book saying they should not be. [rae-0310]

## What everyone gets wrong about Adam Smith

RAM: Okay, I want to do the big ideas. Explain The Wealth of Nations like I'm a teenager.

SMITH: Very well. Why are some nations rich and others poor? Not because they hoard gold. A nation's wealth is what its people can make and enjoy. And the great engine of that is the division of labour. One man alone can scarcely make a pin a day. Ten men in a workshop, each doing one part of the job, made upwards of forty-eight thousand pins a day. [won-0005]

SMITH: But you can only divide work if you can trade what you make. "It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest." We feed each other by trading, not by charity. And the more people you can trade with, the finer the work can be divided, and the richer everyone becomes. [won-0017, won-0020]

RAM: So, and this is what people say about you, greed is good?

SMITH: No. This is the thing I most wish to correct. I said we deal with the butcher through his interest, not his kindness. I never said kindness does not exist, or does not matter. Read the first sentence of my first book.

RAM: Go on.

SMITH: "How selfish soever man may be supposed, there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him, though he derives nothing from it, except the pleasure of seeing it." That is how I began. Before I wrote a word about markets I spent a whole book on sympathy: how we put ourselves in another person's place, and feel a little of what they feel. [tms-0000]

RAM: So the guy everyone quotes on self-interest actually started with compassion.

SMITH: I started with human beings, who are both. A man who is only selfish is a monster, and a man who is only benevolent cannot feed his family. The market works through self-interest. A decent life is impossible without sympathy. I wrote both books, and I meant them to be read together.

RAM: The invisible hand. It's the most famous phrase in economics. People think it means markets fix everything.

SMITH: Do you know how many times I used it in The Wealth of Nations? Once. One time, in a chapter about restraints on foreign trade. I said a merchant who invests for his own gain is often "led by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention." Often, not always. And in the very next breath I said I had never known much good done by those who affected to trade for the public good. [won-0561]

SMITH: I used it in The Theory of Moral Sentiments, once, about how the rich, who consume little more than the poor, end up sharing the necessaries of life. And once, much earlier, in an essay on astronomy, where I mocked the ancients for blaming thunder on "the invisible hand of Jupiter." Three times in a whole life of writing. And from that, they made me a prophet. [tms-0214, essays-0024]

RAM: If the invisible hand isn't your best idea, what is?

SMITH: The impartial spectator. When you are about to wrong someone for your own benefit, what stops you? Not your soft heart. It is "the inhabitant of the breast, the man within, the great judge and arbiter of our conduct." A voice that tells you that you are one of the multitude, in no respect better than any other in it. [tms-0166]

RAM: Give me an example.

SMITH: Suppose the great empire of China were swallowed by an earthquake. A humane man in Europe hears the news. He would express great sorrow, say wise things about the frailty of life, perhaps calculate the effect on trade. Then he would go to bed. "If he was to lose his little finger to-morrow, he would not sleep to-night; but, provided he never saw them, he will snore with the most profound security over the ruin of a hundred millions of his brethren." [tms-0165]

RAM: Oof. That's every one of us scrolling past a disaster on our phones.

SMITH: Is it? Then my book still has readers. But here is the turn. Ask that same man whether he would let a hundred million people die to save his little finger, and he is horrified. He would never do it. Our feelings are small and selfish. Our conscience is not. That gap is the most interesting thing about human beings. [tms-0166]

RAM: You also wrote about America, right as the revolution was starting.

SMITH: I did, and I was not popular for it in London. Britain was spending fortunes defending colonies that paid almost nothing towards their own defence, all to keep a monopoly of their trade. I wrote that the rulers of Great Britain had amused the people with the imagination of a great empire across the Atlantic. "It has hitherto been, not an empire, but the project of an empire; not a gold mine, but the project of a gold mine." [won-1212]

RAM: So what did you tell them to do?

SMITH: Either make the project pay, or give it up. It was time, I said, to awake from "this golden dream" and for Great Britain to accommodate her designs "to the real mediocrity of her circumstances." You can imagine how pleased the government was to read that its empire was mediocre. [won-1212]

RAM: And one underrated idea that should be more famous?

SMITH: That merchants are dangerous when they meet. "People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices." The men who quote me in favour of business rarely quote that. [won-0161]

SMITH: And when such men come to the government asking for a new law, I said it "ought never to be adopted till after having been long and carefully examined, not only with the most scrupulous, but with the most suspicious attention." I was never the friend of businessmen. I was the friend of the buyer. [won-0332]

## Friendship, love and loss

RAM: Let's talk about the people in your life. David Hume. What was he to you?

SMITH: My dearest friend. That is how I began my letters to him, and I meant it. He was cheerful, generous and brave, and he was the finest mind of our age. He was also wonderfully careful with money in small ways. When he was dying he sent me a letter by the common carrier, not the post, and it sat in the carrier's office for eight days. I wrote back: "You had, in order to save me the sum of one penny sterling, sent it by the carrier instead of the Post." [rae-0328]

RAM: He was dying and you scolded him about a penny?

SMITH: That was how we loved each other: by teasing. I told him in the same letter that if I died before his Dialogues were published, I would keep my copy as carefully as if I were to live a hundred years. And when I sent him an enclosed letter to return, I asked him to send it back by the post, and not by the carrier. [rae-0328]

RAM: How did he face death?

SMITH: Better than any man I have known. He had been ill for more than two years, and he "looked at the approach of death with a steady cheerfulness such as very few men have been able to maintain for a few hours." We joked together about what excuse he could give to Charon, the ferryman of the dead, to delay the crossing, and how badly Charon would receive it. [rae-0329]

RAM: And after he died you wrote about him publicly.

SMITH: A letter to our publisher, Strahan, describing his last illness. I ended it by saying that I had always considered him "as approaching as nearly to the idea of a perfectly wise and virtuous man as perhaps the nature of human frailty will permit." [rae-0345]

RAM: And that caused trouble.

SMITH: A storm. David was the most famous unbeliever in Britain, and I had said he lived and died like a good man. People thought it an attack on religion. A clergyman at Oxford wrote a whole pamphlet against me. Even James Boswell, who had been my own student, called it daring effrontery. The joke is that I had braced myself for an outcry over David's Dialogues on religion, and when they came out nobody cared. It was a few pages of kindness to a dead friend that brought the storm. [rae-0345, rae-0346]

RAM: Would you write it again?

SMITH: Every word. A friend's character is not to be adjusted to suit the opinion of the town.

RAM: After Hume, who were your people?

SMITH: In Edinburgh, in my last years, Joseph Black the chemist and James Hutton the geologist. The three of us founded a club that met every week. We called it the Oyster Club, which sounds very grand and greedy, but no three men cared less for food. Hutton drank no wine, and Black lived on bread, a few prunes and milk and water. The conversation was free, never a lecture, and every interesting stranger who came to Edinburgh found his way to us. [rae-0372]

RAM: And you? What was your vice?

SMITH: Sugar. I confess it. Mr Walter Scott, who was a boy then, told the story of a tea party where I would not sit down, but walked round and round the table stealing lumps from the sugar basin, until the lady of the house put the basin on her own knee to protect it from me. [rae-0372]

RAM: The father of economics, banned from the sugar bowl.

SMITH: A clear case of a monopoly protecting itself against an inefficient consumer.

RAM: You never married. Was there ever someone?

SMITH: You promised a love life, Mr Ram, and I fear you will be disappointed. When I was young there was a lady of great beauty and accomplishment. I was attached to her for several years. Nobody ever knew why nothing came of it, and I shall not tell you either. She never married, and neither did I. [rae-0227]

RAM: That's sad. And France?

SMITH: France was comic. At Abbeville there was a marquise, a woman of talent and wit, who had heard in Paris that I was Hume's great friend and nearly as great a philosopher. She resolved to make a conquest of me, and laid siege for days. I could not bear it, and I could not hide my embarrassment, which amused my companions extremely. [rae-0227]

RAM: You just weren't interested?

SMITH: My heart was otherwise engaged. There was an English lady staying at the same hotel. That is all I shall say, and more than I have said to anyone in two hundred years. [rae-0227]

RAM: And your mother.

SMITH: She died in May 1784, in her ninetieth year. I had lived with her for sixty years. It was the first great sorrow of a life that had been, I must admit, singularly smooth. Some people said I grieved too much, as if I had no hope of seeing her again. I sat by her bed when the minister came, and joined in his prayers. Let them make of that what they like. I was never the same afterwards. [rae-0435]

RAM: I'm sorry.

SMITH: Thank you. In my book on the moral sentiments I wrote that adversity depresses the mind much further below its natural state than prosperity can raise it above. I did not understand how true that was until she was gone. [tms-0058]

## Money, success and happiness

RAM: Let's talk about money and success, because a lot of people watching this are young and ambitious. What does chasing wealth do to a person?

SMITH: I wrote a little portrait of such a person. "The poor man's son, whom Heaven in its anger has visited with ambition." He looks around and admires the condition of the rich. His father's cottage is too small. He would like to be carried about in a carriage. He imagines that if only he had all these things, he would sit still contentedly and be happy. [tms-0208]

RAM: That's literally every motivational video on the internet.

SMITH: Then let me finish the story, since your videos apparently stop halfway. To get these things, in the very first year he submits to more fatigue and anxiety than he would have suffered his whole life without them. He works night and day. "He serves those whom he hates, and is obsequious to those whom he despises." [tms-0208, tms-0209]

SMITH: And at the end, in old age, his body worn with toil and disease, he discovers that "wealth and greatness are mere trinkets of frivolous utility." He has spent his life chasing an artificial repose, and for it he "sacrifices a real tranquillity that is at all times in his power." [tms-0209]

RAM: So you're saying don't be ambitious?

SMITH: No. I am saying be honest about what you are buying. The poor man's son also makes the world richer. Nature deceives us into thinking the trinkets will make us happy, and "It is this deception which rouses and keeps in continual motion the industry of mankind." It made us till the ground, build houses and found cities. It is good for the world. It is not always good for him. [tms-0213]

RAM: Then what actually makes people happy?

SMITH: "What can be added to the happiness of the man who is in health, who is out of debt, and has a clear conscience?" Very little. Everything beyond that is decoration. And I said this was the natural and ordinary state of most men. [tms-0058]

RAM: Health, no debt, clear conscience. That's it?

SMITH: Between that condition and the highest pitch of prosperity, the difference is a trifle. Between that condition and real misery, the distance is immense. So guard your health, stay out of debt, and do nothing you would be ashamed to explain. Do that, and the rest is ornament. [tms-0058]

RAM: But you also wrote that the rich and poor aren't that different in happiness. That's hard to accept today, when the gap between rich and poor is enormous.

SMITH: Push me on it. You should. I wrote that "the beggar, who suns himself by the side of the highway, possesses that security which kings are fighting for." I was speaking of peace of mind, not of comfort. But in The Wealth of Nations I said something harder: that the scanty maintenance of the labouring poor is the natural symptom that things are at a stand, and their starving condition that they are going fast backwards. A nation where working people are poorly paid is not a rich nation, however grand its palaces. [tms-0214, won-0091]

RAM: So no billionaire worship from you.

SMITH: I asked why we admire the rich so much. Not because we believe they are happier. "He does not even imagine that they are really happier than other people: but he imagines that they possess more means of happiness." We are dazzled by the machinery, not the result. Respect a man for what he does, not for the number of his contrivances. [tms-0211]

RAM: Advice for a young person starting a career today?

SMITH: Three things. First, find the work you can do better than your neighbours and give your whole attention to it. The tailor does not make his own shoes. And a mind fixed on one object is the mind that invents. Second, distrust any profession that pays you whether or not you do your duty. It will make you lazy, as it made the Oxford professors lazy. Third, keep a friend like David Hume, someone who will tell you the truth and tease you about it. [won-0562, won-0010, won-0964]

## Hot takes: AI, influencers and India

RAM: Hot takes round. I explain something quickly, you react. Ready?

SMITH: I have never been quick in my life, but I will try.

RAM: Artificial intelligence. Machines that can write, draw, answer questions, even do parts of a lawyer's or programmer's job. People are scared it will take their jobs.

SMITH: Let me tell you about a boy. In the first steam engines, a boy was employed to open and close a valve as the piston rose and fell. One of these boys, who loved to play with his companions, tied a string from the handle of the valve to another part of the machine, so it worked by itself. One of the greatest improvements to that engine was "the discovery of a boy who wanted to save his own labour." [won-0010]

RAM: So you're optimistic.

SMITH: I am optimistic about the engine. I would ask what became of the boy. Did he go back to play, or to some better work, or did he lose his place? I also warned that a man who spends his whole life on a few simple operations has no occasion to use his understanding, and becomes dull. If your thinking machines take the simple work and leave people the interesting work, wonderful. If they take the thinking and leave people only the pressing of buttons, then you must take care of their minds. In my day the remedy was a school in every parish. [won-0986, won-0990]

RAM: Next. Social media influencers. People who post pictures of their lives for millions of strangers, and they get paid for attention.

SMITH: For attention alone? Not for any work?

RAM: Often just attention.

SMITH: Then I described them in 1759, without the pictures. There is a difference between the man who wants to be esteemed for what is really estimable and the man who wants praise on any terms. The first is easily satisfied. The second "is never to be satisfied, is full of jealousy and suspicion that we do not esteem him so much as he desires, because he has some secret consciousness that he desires more than he deserves." [tms-0297]

RAM: You just roasted the entire internet.

SMITH: I roasted myself first. Every author wants praise. The question is whether he wants to be praiseworthy.

RAM: Is college worth it? University is incredibly expensive now. A lot of young people come out with huge debts.

SMITH: Huge debts? Then my first question is whether the teachers work for the students' money or are paid regardless. I observed that the parts of education for which there are no public institutions are generally the best taught. A young man who goes to a fencing school "seldom fails of learning to fence." Because the fencing master is paid by his pupils, and if they do not learn, they do not come back. [won-0968]

SMITH: Oxford, rich and endowed, taught me almost nothing. Glasgow, poor, where the professors depended in part on fees, gave me Hutcheson. So I would say: a university is worth exactly as much as it is made to care whether you learn. And if you must borrow to attend, borrow for the teacher, not the name. [won-0964, rae-0012, rae-0054]

RAM: Fake news, conspiracy theories. Misinformation spreads incredibly fast now.

SMITH: In my day we called it enthusiasm and superstition. I proposed two remedies. First, "Science is the great antidote to the poison of enthusiasm and superstition." Second, more surprisingly, public amusements: painting, poetry, music, dancing, plays. Gloomy, melancholy people are the easiest prey for fanatics. Theatre that laughs at fanatics does more than a hundred sermons. [won-1004]

RAM: So your answer to misinformation is science and comedy.

SMITH: Science and comedy. Perhaps even a podcast.

RAM: Crypto. Digital money that no government issues. People buy it hoping it goes up, and some have become very rich and others have lost everything.

SMITH: Money that no bank or king stands behind? Well, remember the nails of Fife. Money is whatever people will accept. So it is not impossible. But I lived through two lessons. The first was a Scotsman, John Law, who believed paper money could be multiplied to almost any extent. France adopted his scheme, and it became the Mississippi scheme, "the most extravagant project, both of banking and stock-jobbing, that perhaps the world ever saw." His "splendid but visionary ideas" still dazzled people in my day. [won-0027, won-0397]

SMITH: The second I saw with my own eyes. In 1772 a great bank in Scotland, Douglas, Heron and Company, founded with the best intentions only three years before, collapsed and shook the whole country. Only three of thirty private banks in Edinburgh survived the crisis. My own pupil, the Duke, was one of its largest shareholders, and with liability unlimited nobody could say how much he would lose. I spent months helping my friends through it. [rae-0276]

RAM: So your advice on crypto?

SMITH: The same as on any splendid new money. Ask who will pay you when you wish to be paid. If the answer is whoever buys it next, you are not holding money. You are holding hope. [won-0397]

RAM: Taxes. Everyone hates them. How should a government tax people?

SMITH: I gave four rules. People should pay in proportion to their abilities, that is, to the income they enjoy under the protection of the state. The tax should be certain, not arbitrary. It should be collected when it is convenient to pay. And it should take as little as possible beyond what reaches the treasury. [won-1048, won-1049]

SMITH: The second rule is the one governments forget. When the tax is uncertain, everyone is at the mercy of the tax-gatherer. I wrote that "a very considerable degree of inequality, it appears, I believe, from the experience of all nations, is not near so great an evil as a very small degree of uncertainty." [won-1049]

RAM: Says the man who became a tax collector.

SMITH: You are enjoying this too much, Mr Ram. We will come to that.

RAM: Last one, and it matters to a lot of our viewers. India. In your time, the British East India Company was taking over large parts of India. What did you think of it?

SMITH: I thought it was a disaster, and I said so. "The government of an exclusive company of merchants is, perhaps, the worst of all governments for any country whatever." A company that rules a country has the power to oppress the people and every temptation to do it. [won-0713]

RAM: You wrote about Bengal specifically.

SMITH: I did. Bengal was a fertile country, where food should not have been hard to find, and yet "three or four hundred thousand people die of hunger in one year." When that happens in a fertile country, I wrote, you may be sure the funds that maintain the labouring poor are fast decaying. That was the state of Bengal under the Company. I set it beside North America, governed under the British constitution, and wrote that the difference between that and "the mercantile company which oppresses and domineers in the East Indies" could not be better illustrated than by the state of the two countries. [won-0090]

RAM: Today India is one of the largest and fastest-growing economies in the world. More than a billion people.

SMITH: A billion! Then here is my test, and it is the same for India as for Scotland. Do not ask how many palaces it has, or how much gold. Ask whether the wages of its working people are rising. "The liberal reward of labour, therefore, as it is the necessary effect, so it is the natural symptom of increasing national wealth." If that is happening for a billion people, it is the greatest thing I have heard today, and I include your telephone. [won-0091]

## Sixteen volumes in the fire

RAM: Let's talk about regrets. In your last years you had a government job. You were a Commissioner of Customs.

SMITH: You see the joke. The man who spent his best book attacking duties on trade spent his last twelve years collecting them. My father was a customs officer, and in the end so was I. I will say this: it taught me a great deal. I told a friend I would never have known or believed how essential practical knowledge was to understanding political subjects. [rae-0365, rae-0367]

RAM: There's a story about you and the doorman at the Customs House.

SMITH: I am told there is. I do not remember it, which my friends found the most remarkable part. Apparently the Board kept a porter in a great scarlet gown, with a staff seven feet high, who saluted each Commissioner at the door. He had saluted me perhaps five hundred times. One morning I saluted him back with my cane, followed him up the stairs, step for step, in exactly his posture, and bowed to him at the top with the utmost gravity. [rae-0366, rae-0367]

RAM: You marched behind him like a soldier.

SMITH: With complete seriousness, I am told. And when I came to myself I had no idea anything had happened. Usually, when my mind wandered at dinner, I could recover long stretches of the conversation afterwards. Not that time. I have no memory of it at all. [rae-0367]

RAM: Here's the real regret question. Before you died, you had your friends burn your papers.

SMITH: Sixteen volumes of manuscript. I was too weak to do it myself, so I begged Black and Hutton, two of my dearest friends, again and again. They kept putting me off, hoping I would recover or change my mind. A week before I died I sent for them and made them do it, there and then, without reading a page. [rae-0480]

RAM: Why? Do you know what historians would give to read those?

SMITH: I know. But only a few of my papers were finished enough to deserve publication, and a man should be judged by the work he chose to finish, not by everything he scribbled. Still, I will be honest with you. I told a friend at the end that I regretted having done so little. I said I meant "to have done more, and there are materials in my papers of which I could have made a great deal." I had promised the world a book on law and government in 1759. I never wrote it. [rae-0480, rae-0310]

RAM: What did you spend your last year on?

SMITH: Not on anything new. On my first book. The Theory of Moral Sentiments had been in the world thirty years and five editions, and I had never revised it. In my last year, though I was very ill, I went back to it and added a great deal. Of all the work I could have finished, I chose to finish that one. [rae-0471]

RAM: That says a lot about which book mattered to you.

SMITH: Draw your own conclusions, Mr Ram. I have drawn mine.

RAM: So the father of economics died feeling unfinished.

SMITH: Every honest man dies unfinished. Only fools die satisfied.

RAM: How do you feel about how you're remembered? Politicians quote you. People put you on ties.

SMITH: On ties! Well. I would ask them to read both of my books, and all of the second. I said the consumer's interest was the end of all production. I said merchants conspire. I said wages should be liberal. I said the poor deserve education at public expense. If a man quotes the invisible hand and none of those, he has borrowed my hat and left the rest of me behind. [won-0833, won-0161, won-0091, won-0990]

## The last question

RAM: We're at the end. One piece of advice for everyone watching.

SMITH: Learn to see yourself as an impartial spectator would see you. Not as your mother sees you, nor your enemies, nor the crowd that applauds you. Ask what a fair-minded stranger would think, watching. Then live so that he would approve. It will not make you rich. It will make you someone worth knowing. [tms-0166]

RAM: Now the show tradition. Every guest leaves a question for our next guest, without knowing who it will be. What's yours?

SMITH: Let me think. Here it is. Was there something you left unfinished? And if your friends offered to save it from the fire, would you let them?

RAM: That's beautiful, and a bit dark.

SMITH: I am a Scotsman, sir. We do beautiful and a bit dark very well.

RAM: Last thing. What were your final words to your friends?

SMITH: It was a Sunday evening. My friends always came to supper on Sundays, and they came in good numbers that night. I went to bed early, and at the door I turned and said, "I love your company, gentlemen, but I believe I must leave you to go to another world." One of them remembers it a little differently: "I believe we must adjourn this meeting to some other place." Perhaps I said both. [rae-0481]
