# Isaac Newton: The Apple Myth, Bitter Rivals and a Mouse Called Miller

> The man who explained the heavens on satellites, a farm he nearly ruined, the plague years at Woolsthorpe, the apple he does not remember, and the rivals he never forgave.

## Welcome to the future

RAM: Welcome to The Deadman Show. I'm Ram. One thing I always say up front: my guest is an AI recreation. Everything he says is built from his own writings and a biography of his life, and every source is listed in the description. When he talks about our world, that's the recreation reasoning from his principles. Today's guest was born on Christmas Day 1642 in Lincolnshire and died in London in 1727. He wrote the Principia and the Opticks, and he is the reason school children everywhere learn about gravity. Sir Isaac Newton, welcome to the show.

NEWTON: Thank you, Mr Ram. You tell me this is the future. I admit nothing that experiment does not show, so I shall wait for the evidence.

RAM: Fair. Here's some evidence. You built a telescope with your own hands. How big was it?

NEWTON: Small. About six inches and a quarter long, with a concave metal where the object-glass should be. I could read at a greater distance with it than with a good glass four feet long. "Two of these I made about 16 Years ago, and have one of them still by me, by which I can prove the truth of what I write." [opticks-0096, opticks-0097]

RAM: Today the biggest telescopes on Earth have mirrors about ten metres across.

NEWTON: I do not know your metres.

RAM: Sorry. A metre is a bit over three feet. So, over thirty feet across.

NEWTON: Thirty feet. Of metal?

RAM: Glass, mostly, with a very thin coat of metal on the front.

NEWTON: Glass! I proposed glass myself, and the London artist who ground it for me made a mess of it. "I satisfied my self that there wants nothing but a good Artist to bring the Design to perfection." It seems the good artist took some time to be born. [opticks-0099]

RAM: Here's the one I've been waiting to tell you. You wrote that even a perfect telescope would be spoiled by the air itself.

NEWTON: I did, and I stand by it. "For the Air through which we look upon the Stars, is in a perpetual Tremor". You see it in the twinkling of the fixed stars. "The only Remedy is a most serene and quiet Air, such as may perhaps be found on the tops of the highest Mountains above the grosser Clouds." [opticks-0102]

RAM: You were right. We build observatories on mountaintops now. But we went further. We put telescopes above the air altogether, circling the Earth out in space, where nothing trembles.

NEWTON: Above the air. And what holds them up there?

RAM: You told us. The leaden ball fired from a mountain.

NEWTON: Ah. I wrote that if you fired a ball from a mountain with a greater and greater velocity, it would land farther and farther away, ten degrees, thirty, ninety, "or even might go quite round the whole earth before it falls; or lastly, so that it might never fall to the earth, but go forward into the celestial spaces, and proceed in its motion in infinitum." It was a thing done on paper, Mr Ram, to show how the Moon is kept in her orbit. [principia-0009, principia-0010]

RAM: We've done it. Thousands of machines are circling the Earth right now. People, too. Men have walked on the Moon.

NEWTON: Give me a moment. A thing I could only reason out, done with the hands. Men standing on the body I spent years trying to calculate. I have never cared for poetry, but that comes very near it. [brewster-0005]

RAM: One more. The comet of 1682. You wrote it might come back.

NEWTON: I wrote it carefully. By Dr. Halley's computation its orbit agreed very nearly with the comet of 1607, so it might return in seventy-five years. "But these things are to be supposed on condition, that, after the space of 75 years, the same comet shall return again in the same orbit." [principia-0774]

RAM: It came back. And today it's called Halley's Comet.

NEWTON: Good. The computation was his. And had it not come back, I suspect you would be calling it Newton's mistake.

RAM: Today we'll cover your childhood, the plague years, the apple, Hooke and Leibniz, the Mint, your faith, and our world. Ready?

NEWTON: When I was a young man I advised a friend who was setting out on his travels. I told him to ask more than he asserted, "it being the designe of travellers to learne, not to teach." I am the traveller now. Ask. [brewster-0303]

## A mouse called the miller

RAM: Let's start at the very beginning. You were born on Christmas Day, and your father was already dead.

NEWTON: He died a few months after he married my mother, before I was born. He was thirty-six. So I never knew him at all. I came early, and I was so small that two women were sent to Lady Pakenham's at North Witham to fetch some medicine to strengthen me, and they did not expect to find me alive when they came back. [brewster-0001]

RAM: That's quite an entrance.

NEWTON: A poor beginning for a man who went on to live past eighty. We were modest people. The manor of Woolsthorpe was worth thirty pounds a year, and with my mother's small estate at Sewstern, about eighty. I was told, later, that I was born a year after Galileo died. People seemed to like saying it. [brewster-0001]

RAM: And then, when you were three, your mother remarried.

NEWTON: To the Reverend Barnabas Smith, the rector of North Witham, about a mile away. She went to live with him, and I was left at Woolsthorpe in the care of her own mother. [brewster-0002]

RAM: A mile away. That's close enough to see the house, but you weren't living in it. How does a three-year-old make sense of that?

NEWTON: You are asking me for my feelings about it, and I wrote none of them down. I will not invent them now to please your audience. I will tell you what happened. What I felt is between me and God.

RAM: That's fair. I'll leave it there. Where did you go to school?

NEWTON: First to two little day-schools, at Skillington and at Stoke. Then, at twelve, to the school at Grantham, under Mr. Stokes. I boarded with Mr. Clark, the apothecary in the town. [brewster-0002]

RAM: And were you the star pupil?

NEWTON: I was very near the bottom. I paid no attention to my lessons at all. [brewster-0002]

RAM: So what changed?

NEWTON: A boy kicked me. He was the boy just above me in the school, and one day he gave me a hard kick in the stomach. It hurt a great deal. So I worked without stopping until I had got above him. And once I had started climbing, I found I could not stop, and I kept on rising until I was the head boy. [brewster-0002]

RAM: That's the greatest revenge story I've ever heard. He kicks you, and you just quietly out-study him.

NEWTON: It was the only sort of revenge a boy like me could take. I was not going to win with my fists.

RAM: But you weren't just studying. I've read you were building things the whole time.

NEWTON: When the other boys were at their games, I was at my tools. Little saws, hatchets, hammers. There was a windmill being built near Grantham, on the road to Gunnerby, and I went and watched the workmen so often that I understood the whole machine. Then I made a working model of it, and we would set it on the roof of the house where I lodged, and the wind turned the sails. [brewster-0003]

RAM: And then you put a mouse in it.

NEWTON: I did. A model that only goes when the wind blows is a poor thing on a still day. So I enclosed a mouse in it, on a sort of treadwheel, and the mouse drove the mill. I called him the miller. [brewster-0003]

RAM: How did you get the mouse to run?

NEWTON: People tell it two ways. Some say I pulled a string tied to his tail. Others say there was corn placed above the wheel, which he was forever trying to reach and never could. I prefer the second. It is the more philosophical arrangement. [brewster-0003]

RAM: A mouse chasing a reward it never gets. Honestly, that's a lot of modern careers.

NEWTON: Then your century has my sympathy and the mouse's.

RAM: Tell me about the kites. Because I think this is my favourite story about you.

NEWTON: I brought paper kites to the school. I took some pains to find their best shapes and proportions, and where the string ought to be fixed, and at how many points. I also made paper lanterns, folded and lit, to light my way to school on dark winter mornings. And on dark nights I tied the lanterns to the tails of the kites and sent them up. The country people saw lights moving in the sky and took them for comets. [brewster-0003, chittenden-0002]

RAM: So the first comets you ever caused panic about were fake ones you made yourself.

NEWTON: That is unkind, and it is accurate. In those days a comet was taken for an omen of disaster. I suppose I was conducting an experiment on the credulity of Lincolnshire.

RAM: What else did you build?

NEWTON: A water clock, about four feet high, made from a box I begged from Mrs. Clark's brother. I filled it every morning, and the Clarks used it as their clock long after I had left Grantham. I also made a carriage with four wheels that moved when the person sitting in it turned a handle. It worked very well, on a smooth floor. On a road it was useless. [brewster-0003]

RAM: An indoor carriage. Okay. And the sundials?

NEWTON: I watched the sun move across the walls and roofs in the yard, and I marked the hours and half-hours with pins. One of my dials the country people called Isaac's dial, and they would look at it to know the time of day. [brewster-0005, chittenden-0002]

RAM: That's amazing. A teenager, and the whole village is telling the time by you.

NEWTON: The sun was doing the work. I only watched it closely, for a long time. That is most of what I have ever done, if you want the truth.

RAM: I read that you drew, too, and wrote poetry.

NEWTON: My room was hung with pictures I drew and framed myself, Mr. Stokes my schoolmaster among them. I will tell you I once said I "excelled in making verses," and I have also been heard to say I had no use for poetry. Both are true. A man may be good at a thing and still think little of it. [brewster-0005]

RAM: There was a girl in that house in Grantham, wasn't there? Miss Storey.

NEWTON: There was. She was two or three years younger than me, and I liked her company far better than that of the boys. I made little tables and cupboards for her dolls. Neither of us had money enough for anything more to come of it. I visited her whenever I was in Lincolnshire, for the rest of my life. [brewster-0004]

RAM: That's lovely. And then at about fifteen, everything stops.

NEWTON: My stepfather died, and my mother came back to Woolsthorpe with the three children of her second marriage. She thought I would be useful on the farm, and it saved money, so she took me out of school. [brewster-0006]

RAM: Be honest with me. How bad a farmer were you?

NEWTON: Very bad. On Saturdays I was sent to Grantham market with an old servant to sell our grain and buy what the house needed. We put up the horses at the Saracen's Head, and the moment the horses were stabled, I was gone, up to my old garret at Mr. Clark's to read his old books, until the servant had done all the business and came to fetch me. Some days I did not even get as far as Grantham. I sat under a hedge by the road with a book until he came back. [brewster-0006]

RAM: And at home?

NEWTON: At home the sheep wandered off, and the cattle trod down the corn, while I read, or built a model, or stood watching a little water-wheel I had made, throwing up spray from the stream. [brewster-0006]

RAM: I mean, the cows were eating the harvest while you watched a toy water-wheel. Your poor mother.

NEWTON: She was a sensible woman. She saw quite soon that I was never going to cultivate the soil, and she sent me back to Grantham school for some months to prepare me for the university. [brewster-0007]

RAM: And who picked Cambridge?

NEWTON: My uncle, the Reverend Mr. Ayscough, rector of Burton Coggles. He had studied at Trinity College himself, and he told my mother that was where I should go. So I went, at about eighteen, knowing less of science than most of the young men I met there. [brewster-0007, brewster-0009]

## Plague, prisms and a litigious lady

RAM: So you get to Trinity knowing less than everyone else. How do you find mathematics?

NEWTON: By way of astrology, I am a little ashamed to say. I wanted to find out whether judicial astrology had any truth in it. It is said that I settled the question by drawing a figure with the help of one or two problems from Euclid, and found it to be folly. [brewster-0009]

RAM: So the first thing mathematics did for you was debunk horoscopes.

NEWTON: People still believe in them, I suppose?

RAM: Millions of them.

NEWTON: Then mathematics has had less success than I hoped.

RAM: Was there anyone at Cambridge who really mattered to you?

NEWTON: Dr. Barrow, the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics. I revised his optical lectures before they were printed, and he thanked me in his preface. In time he resigned that chair in my favour, to give himself to theology. But I should tell you he also passed me over once. [brewster-0010, brewster-0012, chittenden-0014]

RAM: Barrow did?

NEWTON: He did. There was a fellowship at Trinity, and Mr. Uvedale and I stood for it. Dr. Barrow judged us exactly equal, and gave it to Uvedale because he was the senior. [brewster-0011]

RAM: That must have stung.

NEWTON: It was just. Equal is equal, and seniority must count for something. I did not have to enjoy it.

RAM: Then the plague comes, and Cambridge shuts.

NEWTON: The plague drove me home to Woolsthorpe, and I was there through those years. Much of what I did afterward began there. I had the method of fluxions, as I have said, "in the year 1666, or before." I was trying to grind lenses of other figures than spherical, and failing, and so I "procured a triangular glass prism to try therewith the celebrated phenomena of colours." [chittenden-0005, brewster-0013]

RAM: Paint the picture for me. What did you do with the prism?

NEWTON: I darkened my chamber, made a hole in the window-shutter, and let the sunlight through the prism onto the wall. "It was at first," as I put it, "a very pleasing divertisement to view the vivid and intense colours produced thereby". And then the pleasure turned to surprise, because the image ought to have been round, and it was about five times longer than it was broad. [brewster-0015]

RAM: And that bothered you.

NEWTON: It would not let me alone. The thickness of the glass? Flaws in the prism? Were the rays swerving, like a tennis ball struck with an oblique racket? None of it would answer. At last I passed a single colour through a hole in a board, onto a second prism, and found that each colour is bent by its own constant amount. I will explain what that means later, if you will let me. [brewster-0016, brewster-0017]

RAM: We will. But first, the question everyone watching this wants me to ask. The apple. The story is that you were sitting under a tree, an apple fell on your head, and you discovered gravity. Did it happen?

NEWTON: Tell me what you have been told, and I will tell you what I remember.

RAM: That's basically it. The apple falls, the penny drops.

NEWTON: What I remember is the garden at Woolsthorpe, in the plague time, and sitting alone in it and thinking about gravity. The power that makes all bodies descend toward the centre of the earth does not grow weaker on the tops of the highest mountains, nor at the bottom of the deepest mines. So I asked myself: how far up does it go? Might it reach as far as the Moon? And if it did, might that be what holds her in her orbit? [brewster-0115, chittenden-0007]

RAM: So no apple?

NEWTON: I did not say no apple. I said I remember the Moon. Whether something fell in an orchard that afternoon, I cannot swear before you, and I will not. It is the Moon that mattered.

RAM: For what it's worth, after you died the tree in that garden was pointed out as the tree. The one whose apple is supposed to have turned your attention to gravity. A storm brought it down, and part of it was made into a chair.

NEWTON: A chair. So the tree that is said to have taught me about falling ended up holding people up. There is a proposition in that somewhere. [brewster-0289, chittenden-0061]

RAM: So what did you do with the Moon idea?

NEWTON: I compared the periods of the planets with their distances from the sun, and found that if they are held by any power like gravity, it must weaken as the square of the distance. Then I tried the Moon. But I was far from books, and I used the common estimate that a degree of latitude is sixty miles. The answer came out one-sixth too strong. [brewster-0115, brewster-0116]

RAM: Only a sixth! That's close.

NEWTON: Close is not true, Mr Ram. A sixth wrong was enough to throw a doubt over the whole thing. So I put it aside, and I told nobody what I had been doing. [brewster-0116, brewster-0117]

RAM: I want to push you on that. You had the answer, near enough, and you just hid it? For years?

NEWTON: I had a conjecture that failed its test. Some other cause would have had to be added to make it fit, and that cause was beyond the reach of observation. I do not publish what I cannot demonstrate. If more men followed that rule, there would be fewer quarrels in philosophy. [brewster-0116, brewster-0117]

RAM: So what made you go back to it?

NEWTON: Years later, at the Royal Society, I heard of Monsieur Picard's new measurement of a degree, and began the old calculation again with it. As I went on, I could see the answer coming right. I was so agitated that I could not carry it on. I had to hand it to a friend to finish. [brewster-0118]

RAM: That's incredible. All those years, and when the answer finally arrives, your hands are shaking too much to write it down.

NEWTON: I did not say my hands shook. But yes. It is not a thing that happens to a man often.

RAM: Let's go back a step. Before all that, your little telescope makes you famous.

NEWTON: The Royal Society asked to see it, and I was told it had been shown to the King and examined by Sir Christopher Wren and Mr. Hooke. They made me a Fellow, and I promised them "what my poor and solitary endeavours can effect towards the promoting your philosophical designs." [brewster-0021, brewster-0022, brewster-0027]

RAM: And then you sent them your discovery about light.

NEWTON: I called it "the oddest, if not the most considerable detection which hath hitherto been made in the operations of nature." Then the objections began. Mr. Hooke. Monsieur Huygens. Others who had not understood the experiments at all. [brewster-0027, brewster-0028, brewster-0036]

RAM: How did that feel?

NEWTON: I will give you what I wrote to Mr. Leibniz at the time. "I was so persecuted with discussions arising from the publication of my theory of light, that I blamed my own imprudence for parting with so substantial a blessing as my quiet to run after a shadow." [brewster-0041]

RAM: That's a heartbreaking line. Do you still think publishing was a mistake?

NEWTON: I thought so then. I resolved to have nothing more to do with philosophy. As you see, I did not keep the resolution. [brewster-0041]

RAM: Because of Halley.

NEWTON: Dr. Halley came to Cambridge to consult me about the motions of the planets. I told him I had brought the demonstration to perfection, and promised him a copy. That copy grew. It became the Principia. [brewster-0120]

RAM: And then Hooke shows up again.

NEWTON: Mr. Hooke let it be known that I had the notion of the inverse square from him. I wrote Dr. Halley an angry postscript, which I later regretted, and told him I would hold back the third book entirely. "Philosophy is such an impertinently litigious lady, that a man had as good be engaged in lawsuits as have to do with her." [brewster-0121, brewster-0122, brewster-0123]

RAM: You were going to cut the part that explains the whole solar system, because of an argument?

NEWTON: I was going to cut the part that would cause the most argument. There is a difference. But Dr. Halley begged me not to, in the name of the Society, which had resolved to print it at its own expense with Halley seeing it through the press. I gave way. It came out complete in May 1687. [brewster-0123, brewster-0124]

RAM: And after that, you leave Cambridge altogether.

NEWTON: For more than twenty-five years I had scarcely left my college more than a few weeks a year. Then my friend Charles Montague, the Chancellor of the Exchequer, had me made Warden of the Mint, and later I was Master. I went to London. [brewster-0175, brewster-0206, brewster-0207]

RAM: From a college room to the Mint in London. Sir Isaac, did you miss the quiet?

NEWTON: Montague promised me the office had not too much business. He was a fine statesman, Mr Ram, but on that point he was mistaken. I could not even find leisure to prepare a new edition of the Principia. But we will come to the Mint. [brewster-0206, brewster-0210]

## A little moon on a mountain

RAM: You promised me an explanation of the second prism. Explain it like I'm a teenager who's never seen a prism.

NEWTON: Very well. Sunlight comes into my darkened chamber and strikes a prism, which spreads it into a long band of colours. Close behind the prism I set a board with a small hole in it, so only one part of that band could pass. Twelve feet farther on, a second board with a second hole, and behind it a second prism. [opticks-0047]

RAM: So you've picked out one slice of the rainbow and fired it into another prism.

NEWTON: Just so. Then I turned the first prism slowly, to and fro, so that each colour in turn came through both holes, and I marked on the far wall where it landed. The holes never moved, so every colour struck the second glass the same way. And yet the blue was bent more than the red, every time. What the first prism bent most, the second bent most. [opticks-0047, opticks-0048]

RAM: And why does that matter so much?

NEWTON: Because the glass does not make the colours. If it did, the second prism would make new ones. It does not. The colours were already in the sunlight, each with its own fixed degree of bending. The prism serves only to sort them. I will go further. "For the Rays to speak properly are not coloured." A ray has a power to stir up the sensation of red in us, as a trembling string stirs up the sensation of a note. The colour is in the seeing. [opticks-0059, opticks-0111]

RAM: So white light isn't pure at all.

NEWTON: White is the most mixed thing there is. "the whiteness of the Sun's Light is compounded of all the primary Colours mix'd in a due Proportion." Hold white paper among all the colours and it looks white. Block some out and the white is gone. [opticks-0118]

RAM: Did anyone believe you?

NEWTON: Not easily. So I mixed painters' powders, red lead, blue bice and others, into a dull colour like new-cut wood. I rubbed it on the floor in the sunshine, with white paper beside it in the shade, and from a distance the powder looked as white as the paper. "For," as I wrote, "when I was trying this, a friend coming to visit me, I stopped him at the door, and before I told him what the colours were, or what I was doing, I asked him which of the two whites were the best, and wherein they differed!" He said they were both good whites, and he could not tell which was the better. [brewster-0031]

RAM: I love that. Your friend comes round to visit, and before he's through the door he's part of an experiment.

NEWTON: He was not the last. A man who visits a natural philosopher should expect to be useful.

RAM: Let's do motion. Your three laws. Teenager version.

NEWTON: "Every body perseveres in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a right line, unless it is compelled to change that state by forces impressed thereon." A thrown ball would go on for ever, if the air and gravity let it. The planets, in their freer spaces, very nearly do. [principia-0024]

RAM: Second.

NEWTON: "If any force generates a motion, a double force will generate double the motion, a triple force triple the motion". Push twice as hard, get twice the change. [principia-0025]

RAM: And the third is the one everybody half-remembers. Every action has an equal and opposite reaction.

NEWTON: "If you press a stone with your finger, the finger is also pressed by the stone." And if a horse drags a stone on a rope, "the horse (if I may so say) will be equally drawn back towards the stone". [principia-0026]

RAM: Hang on. I've seen horses pull things. The horse moves. The stone moves. Nobody's getting dragged backwards.

NEWTON: The stretched rope tries to shorten itself, so it pulls the horse back as hard as it pulls the stone forward. It hinders the one as much as it helps the other. He feels the rope, I promise you. And the law holds in attractions too. The Earth pulls the stone, and the stone pulls the Earth. [principia-0026]

RAM: Now join it up for me. How do you get from a falling stone to the Moon?

NEWTON: Imagine a very small moon going round the Earth so low that it almost touches the tops of the highest mountains. The force keeping it in its orbit would be very nearly the weight of a stone on that mountain. Stop that moon in its course and it falls, like the stone. If the force holding it were something other than gravity, gravity would pull on it as well, and it would fall with both forces together. Therefore "this little moon at the top of a mountain must either be without gravity, or fall twice as swiftly as heavy bodies are wont to do." Neither is believable. So it is one force. [principia-0581]

RAM: That's elegant. And this is the same force on everything? A feather, a cannonball?

NEWTON: I tested it. "I tried the thing in gold, silver, lead, glass, sand, common salt, wood, water, and wheat." Two round wooden boxes, equal in size, hung on threads of eleven feet. One I filled with wood, and in the other I hung an equal weight of gold. "I observed them to play together forward and backward, for a long time, with equal vibrations." [principia-0585]

RAM: Wheat. You put wheat in a pendulum.

NEWTON: I was raised on a farm, Mr Ram. I had to get some use from it eventually.

RAM: And the inverse square. In one sentence.

NEWTON: Double the distance, and the pull is a quarter. But I will confess something. A planet's pull is made up of the pulls of all its parts, and near the surface those parts lie at very unequal distances. "I was yet in doubt whether that reciprocal duplicate proportion did accurately hold, or but nearly so". I had to prove it before I believed it, and I did. [principia-0592]

RAM: Here's the misconception. A lot of people watching think you explained what gravity actually is. You found it, you named it, case closed.

NEWTON: Then they have read me more generously than I wrote. In a later edition of the Principia I said it plainly: "Hitherto we have explained the phænomena of the heavens and of our sea by the power of gravity, but have not yet assigned the cause of this power." I know how it acts, and its measure. What it is, I do not know. "But hitherto I have not been able to discover the cause of those properties of gravity from phænomena, and I frame no hypotheses". [principia-0783]

RAM: But surely you had a guess.

NEWTON: I had questions. In the Opticks I wrote: "What I call Attraction may be perform'd by impulse, or by some other means unknown to me." It is a word for the effect, not the cause. I put my thoughts on the cause as a question, because I am not yet satisfied about it, for want of experiments. [opticks-0320, opticks-0002]

RAM: Let me push you. Leibniz said this was exactly your problem. That you were bringing back the old occult qualities. A pull nobody can explain, working across empty space.

NEWTON: He mistook what I said. "For these are manifest Qualities, and their Causes only are occult." That bodies fall and planets keep their courses, anyone can see. The cause is hidden. To invent a hidden quality and call it an explanation is to tell us nothing. To find the law and leave the cause to be found is a great step. [brewster-0171, opticks-0341]

RAM: Last one. Which idea of yours is underrated?

NEWTON: The questions at the end of the Opticks. I meant to repeat my last experiments with more care. "But I was then interrupted, and cannot now think of taking these things into farther Consideration." So "I shall conclude with proposing only some Queries, in order to a farther search to be made by others." [opticks-0287]

RAM: So you ended your book with homework.

NEWTON: I ended it honestly. Whether the rays are small bodies. Whether particles have powers acting at a distance. Whether rays passing the edge of a body bend back and forth "with a motion like that of an Eel". [opticks-0287, opticks-0315, opticks-0320]

RAM: Like an eel.

NEWTON: I put it as a question, Mr Ram. I do not say the light is an eel.

## Diamond, Locke and a very odd letter

RAM: Let's talk about the people in your life. Who were your real friends?

NEWTON: Dr. Halley first, for without him there is no Principia. I said so in the preface: "it was to his solicitations that its becoming public is owing". When I agreed to keep the title after all, I told him "It will help the sale of the book, which I ought not to diminish now ’tis yours." [principia-0002, brewster-0123]

RAM: Was that a joke? Sir Isaac Newton making a joke about book sales?

NEWTON: It happens about once a decade. You were fortunate in your timing.

RAM: But you two didn't agree on everything.

NEWTON: Not on religion. When Halley spoke lightly of it, I checked him. "I have studied these things,—you have not." He did not stop being my friend, and I did not stop correcting him. [brewster-0285]

RAM: And Charles Montague. He's the one who got you to London.

NEWTON: Montague was twenty years younger than I, a disciple more than an equal at first. In 1684 we tried to found a philosophical society at Cambridge together, and it failed for want of persons willing to try experiments. He rose fast: the Treasury, then Chancellor of the Exchequer. [brewster-0204]

RAM: I read you fell out with him before the Mint job.

NEWTON: I did. I believed he had failed me over an office I hoped for, and I wrote to Mr. Locke, "Being fully convinced that Mr. Montague, upon an old grudge which I thought had been worn out, is false to me, I have done with him". Some years later that same false friend made me Warden of the Mint. [brewster-0192, brewster-0206]

RAM: So you'd "done with him", and he got you the job anyway.

NEWTON: Yes. It does not show me in the best light. Write it down as an experiment that disproved my hypothesis.

RAM: You never married. There was Miss Storey in Grantham, who you've already told us about. Was there anyone else?

NEWTON: You will find no letter of mine on marriage, and I will not compose one now. Miss Storey married twice; I never did. I helped her with her small money troubles for the rest of my life. That is what I can tell you. [brewster-0004, chittenden-0003]

RAM: In London, your niece Catherine Barton ran your house. What was she like?

NEWTON: My niece, educated at my expense, and the widow of Colonel Barton. She kept my house for the last twenty years of my life, and she and her husband, Mr. Conduit, lived with me there. Lord Halifax, Montague as he became, was very attached to her. Why they never married I cannot say. In his will he left her a large part of his fortune, and he left me a hundred pounds. [brewster-0270, brewster-0208]

RAM: A hundred pounds. Ouch.

NEWTON: It was a just valuation. She was better company.

RAM: I have to ask you about a story every schoolchild hears. You had a little dog called Diamond. You left him in your study, he knocked over a candle, and years of your work went up in flames. And you said, "Oh, Diamond, Diamond, little do you know the mischief you have done me!"

NEWTON: I do not recognise the dog, Mr Ram, nor those words. There was talk of papers of mine burned by a candle on a winter morning while I was at chapel, and I do not deny that I lost papers in my life. But you will not find me speaking of experiments lost that way, then or after. A tale that grows a dog and a speech in the retelling is the kind of thing I was taught to distrust. [brewster-0177, brewster-0183]

RAM: Then let's talk about something you did write. In September 1693 you sent two letters, one to Samuel Pepys and one to John Locke, that alarmed both of them.

NEWTON: I know the ones you mean. To Mr. Pepys I wrote, "I am extremely troubled at the embroilment I am in, and have neither ate nor slept well this twelvemonth, nor have my former consistency of mind." And I told him I must withdraw from his acquaintance, and see neither him nor the rest of my friends any more. [brewster-0186]

RAM: And to Locke?

NEWTON: Worse. I had persuaded myself that he was working against me. I wrote, "Being of opinion that you endeavoured to embroil me with women, and by other means, I was so much affected with it, as that when one told me you were sickly and would not live, I answered, ’twere better if you were dead. I desire you to forgive me this uncharitableness". [brewster-0194]

RAM: That's hard to hear. Can I ask what was happening to you?

NEWTON: I can tell you what I told him a few weeks later. "The last winter, by sleeping too often by my fire, I got an ill habit of sleeping; and a distemper, which this summer has been epidemical, put me farther out of order, so that when I wrote to you, I had not slept an hour a night for a fortnight together, and for five days together not a wink." I remembered that I had written to him. I could not remember what I had said. [brewster-0198]

RAM: Some people have read those letters and said you lost your mind.

NEWTON: I lost my sleep. A man who has not slept for five nights is not himself, and he says things he must afterwards answer for. When I met Mr. Millington at Huntingdon, I told him before he could ask that I had written Mr. Pepys a very odd letter, and that I was ashamed of it. [brewster-0189]

RAM: And what did Locke say?

NEWTON: That he forgave me, freely and fully, and wished only for a chance to show me that nothing had changed between us. I signed my letter to him as his "most humble and unfortunate servant". I was both. He was the better man that autumn. [brewster-0194, brewster-0196]

RAM: I think a lot of people watching this will find that the most human thing you've said today. The man who explained the heavens, asking a friend's pardon because he hadn't slept.

NEWTON: The heavens are easier. They do not need to forgive you.

RAM: And you went back to work?

NEWTON: The next autumn I was at Greenwich taking Mr. Flamstead's observations of the Moon, though he treated my requests as if they were a nuisance to him. The Moon, at least, was willing. [brewster-0200]

RAM: People call you a loner. Is that fair?

NEWTON: I had friends and I lost some through my own fault. But if you ask what I wanted most, I told Mr. Locke once: "My inclinations are to sit still." [brewster-0192]

## Lace, lucre and the ocean of truth

RAM: Let's talk money. At fifty-three you were still living on a professor's salary. Then you go to the Mint, and eventually you're Master, on twelve or fifteen hundred pounds a year. Did money change you?

NEWTON: It changed my house. In London I kept a carriage, three men servants and three women. I gave dinners when the occasion called for them. But I ate plainly and dressed plainly. My table was broth, vegetables and fruit. [brewster-0203, brewster-0207, brewster-0285, brewster-0270]

RAM: Always plainly?

NEWTON: Once, not. In 1705 I stood again for the University in Parliament, and I am said to have put on a suit of laced clothes for it. I had been knighted by the Queen at Trinity that same spring. I lost by a very great majority. [brewster-0285, brewster-0209]

RAM: So the one time you dressed up, you got beaten.

NEWTON: Badly. I have drawn no general law from it. One observation is not an induction.

RAM: What did the Mint job actually involve?

NEWTON: The coin of the nation had been adulterated and debased, and Montague resolved to make it all again. My mathematics and my knowledge of chymistry were of use, and the whole recoinage was done in two years. Later I wrote a report on the coinage and drew up a table of the assays of foreign coins. [brewster-0204, brewster-0207]

RAM: And the professorship?

NEWTON: When I became Master, I made Mr. Whiston my deputy at Cambridge, and gave him all the money from the chair. In 1703 I resigned it and got him appointed in my place. [brewster-0207]

RAM: Your friends say you gave money away constantly.

NEWTON: To my relations, a great deal, and to the poor, and to learning. When I was past eighty I offered twenty pounds a year toward a place for young Mr. Maclaurin at Edinburgh. I used to say that those who give nothing away until they die have never really given at all. A legacy costs the giver nothing. [brewster-0286]

RAM: That's a good line. I want to push you on something, though. You say you hated disputes. You sat on the Opticks for years.

NEWTON: "To avoid being engaged in Disputes about these Matters, I have hitherto delayed the printing, and should still have delayed it, had not the Importunity of Friends prevailed upon me." [opticks-0000]

RAM: But when Leibniz said you'd copied him, you went to war. For people watching: you and Leibniz both developed what we now call calculus. You called it fluxions. Leibniz said you didn't have his method before him. Historians today generally say you both found it independently. And some historians say you had a bigger hand in the Royal Society's report on the dispute than you let on.

NEWTON: I said before the Society that I did not interfere in that publication, and that I held back two letters which favoured me. I stand on what I said then. I would not answer his letter to the Abbé Conti, whatever my friends urged, until the King himself asked when my answer would appear. And then I wrote, "But he is the aggressor, and it lies upon him to prove the charge." [brewster-0170, brewster-0171]

RAM: That doesn't sound like a man who values his quiet.

NEWTON: A man may love his quiet and still not let himself be called a thief. He accused me of plagiary. That is a charge against my honesty, not my mathematics. In the Opticks I published what I had tried, and left the rest to others. [brewster-0171, opticks-0000]

RAM: So what actually made you happy? Not money, not winning.

NEWTON: I never wrote down a rule for happiness, so I will not pretend to one. I can tell you what I valued. My quiet. And the work itself. "And though every true Step made in this Philosophy brings us not immediately to the Knowledge of the first Cause, yet it brings us nearer to it, and on that account is to be highly valued." [brewster-0041, opticks-0315]

RAM: Your household said you'd sit on the edge of your bed for hours in the morning without getting dressed.

NEWTON: Thinking. And they had to remind me to eat. People called it genius. I always said that whatever I had done for the public was owing to no extraordinary sagacity, only to industry and patient thought. [brewster-0286, brewster-0277]

RAM: There's a line of yours, from near the end of your life. A lot of people watching this will know it.

NEWTON: The boy on the shore. I said it to someone, not long before I died, and I still mean it. I do not know how I look to the world. To myself I seem only a boy playing by the sea, pleased now and then to find a smoother pebble or a prettier shell than the others, "while the great ocean of truth lay all undiscovered before me." [brewster-0284]

RAM: That might be the humblest thing anyone famous has ever said.

NEWTON: It is not humility. It is measurement. Set what I found beside what is left to find, and see how small the one is.

RAM: Last thing. A young person starting their career today. What do you tell them?

NEWTON: What I told young Mr. Aston when I was twenty-six and he was setting out on his travels. "You will find little or no advantage in seeming wiser or much more ignorant than your company." And, "If you bee affronted, it is better, in a forraine country, to pass it by in silence, and with a jest, though with some dishonour, than to endeavour revenge". [brewster-0302, brewster-0303]

RAM: With respect, Sir Isaac, you did not follow that advice. Hooke. Leibniz.

NEWTON: No. It is easier to advise a traveller than to be one. But the rule was sound, even if the man who wrote it was not always. "In a word, if you can keep reason above passion, that and watchfullnesse will be your best defendants." [brewster-0303]

RAM: Anything else from that letter?

NEWTON: I told him to watch for metals changed into other metals in the mines abroad, those being "the most luciferous, and many times lucriferous experiments too, in philosophy." [brewster-0304]

RAM: Translate that for me.

NEWTON: Light-bringing, and money-bringing. Look first for the work that shows you something. Sometimes it pays as well. Rarely the other way round.

RAM: Light first, money second. I like that. Next, I'm going to throw some of our world at you, fast. And for anyone who's just tuned in: my guest is an AI recreation of Sir Isaac Newton, built from his own writings and a biography of his life.

## Clocks, comets and the end of the world

RAM: First one. You gave evidence to the Commons committee on finding longitude at sea. Today, the phone in my pocket knows where I am on the Earth to within a few feet. It does it by listening to clocks circling overhead.

NEWTON: Clocks. Then you solved the watch. In 1714 I told the committee of the Commons that a watch to keep time exactly at sea, with the ship rolling, the heat and cold, the wet and dry, and gravity itself different from one latitude to another, "such a watch hath not yet been made." [brewster-0214, brewster-0215]

RAM: Someone made it. A clockmaker, a few decades after your death. And now the clocks are so good we put them in the sky.

NEWTON: I am glad to be wrong in that direction. I said "hath not yet", you will notice. Not never. [brewster-0215]

RAM: Here's a harder one. Nearly two hundred years after you, a man called Einstein showed that your laws aren't quite right. At enormous speeds, or near enormous masses, they drift off. We still use your laws to fly to the Moon. But at the edges, they've been replaced. Does that sting?

NEWTON: Let me read you the fourth of my Rules of Reasoning, and you may judge whether I left the door open. "In experimental philosophy we are to look upon propositions collected by general induction from phænomena as accurately or very nearly true, notwithstanding any contrary hypotheses that may be imagined, till such time as other phænomena occur, by which they may either be made more accurate, or liable to exceptions." [principia-0566]

RAM: So you wrote your own get-out clause.

NEWTON: I wrote the method. Induction "be no Demonstration of general Conclusions; yet it is the best way of arguing which the Nature of Things admits of". If observation has found exceptions, the law must now be spoken with exceptions. I said in my preface I hoped my principles would give "some light either to this or some truer method of philosophy." It seems they did. [opticks-0344, principia-0002]

RAM: Okay. Here's the surprise topic. If you search your name online, one of the first things you'll find is the claim that Isaac Newton predicted the world will end in 2060. Did you?

NEWTON: I will not affirm any date I did not publish, and in what I prepared for the press I set none. Whatever private reckonings I made on loose sheets, I do not stand behind them as prophecy, and I will not do sums now to please your century. Hear what my Observations say about men who set dates. "The folly of Interpreters has been, to foretel times and things by this Prophecy, as if God designed to make them Prophets. By this rashness they have not only exposed themselves, but brought the Prophecy also into contempt." [daniel-0179]

RAM: But you spent years on the prophecies. Surely you wanted to know when.

NEWTON: God gave the prophecies "not to gratify men's curiosities by enabling them to foreknow things, but that after they were fulfilled they might be interpreted by the event". Where a prophecy was not yet fulfilled, I refused to interpret it. "The manner I know not. Let time be the Interpreter." [daniel-0179, daniel-0092]

RAM: So the man the internet says set a date for the end of the world wrote, in print, that date-setting is folly. Though historians say the number does turn up in your private notes.

NEWTON: In print, where I meant it to stand. A man tries sums on loose sheets; he does not swear to them. But I will make you a confession. Toward the end, sitting by a wood fire at Kensington, my nephew Mr. Conduit had me talking about the comet of 1680. It passed so near the sun that it must be slowed, and I had written that it "will at last fall down upon the body of the sun." [principia-0775, brewster-0307]

RAM: And then what?

NEWTON: I told him, as a conjecture, and affirming nothing, that when it falls it may so heat the sun that this earth would be burnt and nothing living could survive on it. When, I could not say. Perhaps five or six more revolutions. Mr. Conduit asked why I would not publish my conjectures, as Kepler did. I told him, "I do not deal in conjectures." [brewster-0307, brewster-0309]

RAM: Right after giving him a conjecture about the end of the world.

NEWTON: He noticed that too. I told him I had said enough for people to know my meaning, and I laughed. A man is allowed one inconsistency by his own fireside. [brewster-0309]

RAM: Next. Many people today think science has made God unnecessary. You explained the heavens with mathematics, so what's left for Him to do?

NEWTON: They have read the first half of my book and not the end. "This most beautiful system of the sun, planets, and comets, could only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent and powerful Being." "Was the Eye contrived without Skill in Opticks, and the Ear without Knowledge of Sounds?" [principia-0779, opticks-0315]

RAM: But you've just told me your laws stand only until better evidence comes. Why doesn't that apply to God?

NEWTON: A fair push. I reasoned to Him from the appearances of things, as I reasoned to gravity. To discourse of God "from the appearances of things, does certainly belong to Natural Philosophy." Your century may weigh the argument. It may not tell me it does not belong in the book. [principia-0783]

RAM: Last one, and I think you'll like it. We've now found thousands of planets going round other stars.

NEWTON: Thousands. I took the fixed stars to be suns, giving light to other planets as ours does to us. In the Opticks I said God might vary the laws of Nature, "and make Worlds of several sorts in several Parts of the Universe. At least, I see nothing of Contradiction in all this." [brewster-0308, opticks-0344]

RAM: So you called it.

NEWTON: I did not call it. I said it was not contradictory. Your astronomers did the observing. Have they found anyone living on them?

RAM: Not yet.

NEWTON: Then they are wise to say so. Let time be the Interpreter there also.

## Left imperfect

RAM: Let's slow down. You've told me what you got right. What did you leave undone?

NEWTON: The third book of the Opticks, to begin with. I said so in the front of it. "The Subject of the Third Book I have also left imperfect, not having tried all the Experiments which I intended when I was about these Matters". I had "only begun the Analysis", and left the hints to be improved "by the farther Experiments and Observations of such as are inquisitive." [opticks-0000, opticks-0345]

RAM: And the cause of gravity, which you've already told us you never found.

NEWTON: That, and a thing I suspected beneath it. At the end of the Principia I wrote of "a certain most subtle Spirit which pervades and lies hid in all gross bodies". Then I stopped, because "nor are we furnished with that sufficiency of experiments which is required to an accurate determination and demonstration of the laws by which this electric and elastic Spirit operates." [principia-0784]

RAM: Electric. You wrote electric?

NEWTON: I did. Why does that make you smile?

RAM: Because almost everything in our world now runs on electricity. You walked right up to the door and decided not to knock.

NEWTON: I had not the experiments to knock with. I would sooner leave a door shut than describe a room I had never seen.

RAM: Here's my push. You told us earlier you were only a boy picking up pebbles. But you also fought Hooke and Leibniz over who got there first. That isn't how a man who doesn't care about credit behaves.

NEWTON: I never said I did not care. I said what I had found was small beside what was left. As to how I wished to be read, it is in the preface to the Principia: "I heartily beg that what I have here done may be read with candour; and that the defects in a subject so difficult be not so much reprehended as kindly supplied, and investigated by new endeavours of my readers." Fix my defects; do not merely sneer at them. [principia-0002]

RAM: Can I ask about your last years?

NEWTON: You may. At eighty I was told I had the stone, and that it could not be cured. I gave up my carriage for a chair and declined all dinners. After a bad cough they moved me to Kensington, with some difficulty, and it did me good. [brewster-0270]

RAM: But you kept going into London.

NEWTON: At the end of February 1727 I went up to preside at the Royal Society. I told my nephew I had slept the night through, from eleven till eight. It was a boast I paid for. The pain came back violently, and by the fourth of March I was at Kensington again, and Dr. Mead and Dr. Cheselden gave no hope. [brewster-0271]

RAM: Your nephew's account says that in the worst of it, with the sweat running down your face, you never cried out or complained. And between the fits you smiled and talked.

NEWTON: Complaining does not dissolve a stone. On the Saturday I read the newspapers and had a long talk with Dr. Mead. After that I have nothing to tell you. You will have to tell me. [brewster-0271]

RAM: You died on the twentieth of March, 1727, and you were buried in Westminster Abbey, with the Lord Chancellor, two dukes and three earls carrying the pall. There's a monument, with carved boys holding your discoveries: a prism, a reflecting telescope, the sun and planets weighed on a scale, a furnace, and coins fresh from the Mint.

NEWTON: The coins. Of course they put the Mint on it. Thirty years of my life, and two stone boys carrying it. That is about right. [brewster-0272]

RAM: And on the wall of the room where you were born, someone later put two lines of verse. "Nature and Nature's Laws were hid in night, God said, 'Let NEWTON be,' and all was light."

NEWTON: That is a line which belongs to God, given to a man. I would have struck it out. And it rhymes, which does not help its case with me. [chittenden-0062]

## The question from the fire

RAM: Every guest on this show leaves a question for the next one, without knowing who'll answer it. Our last guest was Adam Smith, and here is what he left for you. "Was there something you left unfinished? And if your friends offered to save it from the fire, would you let them?"

NEWTON: Mr. Smith asks the right man. You have just heard my unfinished things. The largest of the rest was my Chronology of the ancient kingdoms. Critics in France attacked it, and my friends prevailed on me to prepare the whole for the press. I had nearly finished it. [brewster-0222]

RAM: And would you let them save it?

NEWTON: I have been saved from myself twice already, as you have heard. Dr. Halley would not let me cut the third book of the Principia, and my friends would not let me sit on the Opticks for ever. Both times they were right. [brewster-0124, opticks-0000]

RAM: So the answer is yes.

NEWTON: Not always. Once I stopped a friend from saving me. I had written a tract on two corrupted texts of Scripture, and I myself asked Mr. Locke to have it translated and published abroad. He sent it to Holland to be printed without my name. When I heard it was going ahead, I wrote to him: "Let me entreat you to stop their translation and impression so soon as you can; for I design to suppress them." I was afraid of being drawn into another controversy. [brewster-0230, brewster-0231]

RAM: So which Newton should Adam Smith believe?

NEWTON: Both. I would let them save what was finished and true. What was half-done I would rather they kept back. "If any other Papers writ on this Subject are got out of my Hands they are imperfect". But if what I wrote may help those who come after, then as I said of my work on the prophecies, "if I have done any thing which may be useful to following writers, I have my design." [opticks-0000, brewster-0237]

RAM: For what it's worth, nobody burned your papers. Most of them passed down through your family for two centuries. Many were sold at auction in 1936 and scattered, but they survive.

NEWTON: Then my friends had their way in the end. They usually did.

RAM: One piece of advice for everyone watching.

NEWTON: The first of my Rules. "Nature is pleased with simplicity, and affects not the pomp of superfluous causes." When something goes wrong in your life, do not reach first for the grand explanation, the conspiracy or the curse. Look for the plain cause that is true and sufficient. It is usually there. [principia-0562]

RAM: That's good. Now, your question for our next guest. You don't know who it is.

NEWTON: I ended the Opticks by handing my questions to strangers, so I am used to this. Here is mine, built on my fourth Rule. What did you once believe on good evidence that later evidence proved false, and how long did it take you to admit it? I will answer first, to be fair, though mine runs the other way. I once put aside a true idea because of a false measurement of the Earth, and it was years before better evidence made me take it out again. [principia-0566, brewster-0116, brewster-0118]

RAM: Sir Isaac Newton, thank you. This has been an honour.

NEWTON: You asked more than you asserted, Mr Ram. I advised the same to a traveller once. Thank you.
