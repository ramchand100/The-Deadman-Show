# Isaac Newton: The Apple Myth, Bitter Rivals and a Mouse Called Miller

> The man who explained the heavens on satellites, a farm he nearly ruined, the plague years at Woolsthorpe, the apple he does not remember, and the rivals he never forgave.

## Welcome to the future

RAM: Welcome to The Deadman Show. I'm Ram. One thing I always say up front: my guest is an AI recreation. Everything he says is built from his own writings and a biography of his life, and every source is listed in the description. When he talks about our world, that's the recreation reasoning from his principles. Today's guest was born on Christmas Day 1642 in Lincolnshire and died in London in 1727. He wrote the Principia and the Opticks, and he is the reason school children everywhere learn about gravity. Sir Isaac Newton, welcome to the show.

NEWTON: Thank you, Mr Ram. You tell me this is the future. I admit nothing that experiment does not show, so I shall wait for the evidence.

RAM: Fair. Here's some evidence. You built a telescope with your own hands. How big was it?

NEWTON: Small. About six inches and a quarter long, with a concave metal where the object-glass should be. I could read at a greater distance with it than with a good glass four feet long. "Two of these I made about 16 Years ago, and have one of them still by me, by which I can prove the truth of what I write." [opticks-0096, opticks-0097]

RAM: Today the biggest telescopes on Earth have mirrors about ten metres across.

NEWTON: I do not know your metres.

RAM: Sorry. A metre is a bit over three feet. So, over thirty feet across.

NEWTON: Thirty feet. Of metal?

RAM: Glass, mostly, with a very thin coat of metal on the front.

NEWTON: Glass! I proposed glass myself, and the London artist who ground it for me made a mess of it. "I satisfied my self that there wants nothing but a good Artist to bring the Design to perfection." It seems the good artist took some time to be born. [opticks-0099]

RAM: Here's the one I've been waiting to tell you. You wrote that even a perfect telescope would be spoiled by the air itself.

NEWTON: I did, and I stand by it. "For the Air through which we look upon the Stars, is in a perpetual Tremor". You see it in the twinkling of the fixed stars. "The only Remedy is a most serene and quiet Air, such as may perhaps be found on the tops of the highest Mountains above the grosser Clouds." [opticks-0102]

RAM: You were right. We build observatories on mountaintops now. But we went further. We put telescopes above the air altogether, circling the Earth out in space, where nothing trembles.

NEWTON: Above the air. And what holds them up there?

RAM: You told us. The leaden ball fired from a mountain.

NEWTON: Ah. I wrote that if you fired a ball from a mountain with a greater and greater velocity, it would land farther and farther away, ten degrees, thirty, ninety, "or even might go quite round the whole earth before it falls; or lastly, so that it might never fall to the earth, but go forward into the celestial spaces, and proceed in its motion in infinitum." It was a thing done on paper, Mr Ram, to show how the Moon is kept in her orbit. [principia-0009]

RAM: We've done it. Thousands of machines are circling the Earth right now. People, too. Men have walked on the Moon.

NEWTON: Give me a moment. A thing I could only reason out, done with the hands. Men standing on the body I spent years trying to calculate. I have never cared for poetry, but that comes very near it. [principia-0010]

RAM: One more. The comet of 1682. You wrote it might come back.

NEWTON: I wrote it carefully. By Dr. Halley's computation its orbit agreed very nearly with the comet of 1607, so it might return in seventy-five years. "But these things are to be supposed on condition, that, after the space of 75 years, the same comet shall return again in the same orbit." [principia-0774]

RAM: It came back. And today it's called Halley's Comet.

NEWTON: Good. The computation was his. And had it not come back, I suspect you would be calling it Newton's mistake.

RAM: Today we'll cover your childhood, the plague years, the apple, Hooke and Leibniz, the Mint, your faith, and our world. Ready?

NEWTON: When I was a young man I advised a friend who was setting out on his travels. I told him to ask more than he asserted, "it being the designe of travellers to learne, not to teach." I am the traveller now. Ask. [brewster-0303]

## A mouse called the miller

RAM: Let's start at the very beginning. You were born on Christmas Day, and your father was already dead.

NEWTON: He died a few months after he married my mother, before I was born. He was thirty-six. So I never knew him at all. I came early, and I was so small that two women were sent to Lady Pakenham's at North Witham to fetch some medicine to strengthen me, and they did not expect to find me alive when they came back. [brewster-0001]

RAM: That's quite an entrance.

NEWTON: A poor beginning for a man who went on to live past eighty. We were modest people. The manor of Woolsthorpe was worth thirty pounds a year, and with my mother's small estate at Sewstern, about eighty. I was told, later, that I was born a year after Galileo died. People seemed to like saying it. [brewster-0001]

RAM: And then, when you were three, your mother remarried.

NEWTON: To the Reverend Barnabas Smith, the rector of North Witham, about a mile away. She went to live with him, and I was left at Woolsthorpe in the care of her own mother. [brewster-0002]

RAM: A mile away. That's close enough to see the house, but you weren't living in it. How does a three-year-old make sense of that?

NEWTON: You are asking me for my feelings about it, and I wrote none of them down. I will not invent them now to please your audience. I will tell you what happened. What I felt is between me and God.

RAM: That's fair. I'll leave it there. Where did you go to school?

NEWTON: First to two little day-schools, at Skillington and at Stoke. Then, at twelve, to the school at Grantham, under Mr. Stokes. I boarded with Mr. Clark, the apothecary in the town. [brewster-0002]

RAM: And were you the star pupil?

NEWTON: I was very near the bottom. I paid no attention to my lessons at all. [brewster-0002]

RAM: So what changed?

NEWTON: A boy kicked me. He was the boy just above me in the school, and one day he gave me a hard kick in the stomach. It hurt a great deal. So I worked without stopping until I had got above him. And once I had started climbing, I found I could not stop, and I kept on rising until I was the head boy. [brewster-0002]

RAM: That's the greatest revenge story I've ever heard. He kicks you, and you just quietly out-study him.

NEWTON: It was the only sort of revenge a boy like me could take. I was not going to win with my fists.

RAM: But you weren't just studying. I've read you were building things the whole time.

NEWTON: When the other boys were at their games, I was at my tools. Little saws, hatchets, hammers. There was a windmill being built near Grantham, on the road to Gunnerby, and I went and watched the workmen so often that I understood the whole machine. Then I made a working model of it, and we would set it on the roof of the house where I lodged, and the wind turned the sails. [brewster-0003]

RAM: And then you put a mouse in it.

NEWTON: I did. A model that only goes when the wind blows is a poor thing on a still day. So I enclosed a mouse in it, on a sort of treadwheel, and the mouse drove the mill. I called him the miller. [brewster-0003]

RAM: How did you get the mouse to run?

NEWTON: People tell it two ways. Some say I pulled a string tied to his tail. Others say there was corn placed above the wheel, which he was forever trying to reach and never could. I prefer the second. It is the more philosophical arrangement. [brewster-0003]

RAM: A mouse chasing a reward it never gets. Honestly, that's a lot of modern careers.

NEWTON: Then your century has my sympathy and the mouse's.

RAM: Tell me about the kites. Because I think this is my favourite story about you.

NEWTON: I brought paper kites to the school. I took some pains to find their best shapes and proportions, and where the string ought to be fixed, and at how many points. I also made paper lanterns, folded and lit, to light my way to school on dark winter mornings. And on dark nights I tied the lanterns to the tails of the kites and sent them up. The country people saw lights moving in the sky and took them for comets. [brewster-0003, chittenden-0002]

RAM: So the first comets you ever caused panic about were fake ones you made yourself.

NEWTON: That is unkind, and it is accurate. In those days a comet was taken for an omen of disaster. I suppose I was conducting an experiment on the credulity of Lincolnshire.

RAM: What else did you build?

NEWTON: A water clock, about four feet high, made from a box I begged from Mrs. Clark's brother. I filled it every morning, and the Clarks used it as their clock long after I had left Grantham. I also made a carriage with four wheels that moved when the person sitting in it turned a handle. It worked very well, on a smooth floor. On a road it was useless. [brewster-0003]

RAM: An indoor carriage. Okay. And the sundials?

NEWTON: I watched the sun move across the walls and roofs in the yard, and I marked the hours and half-hours with pins. One of my dials the country people called Isaac's dial, and they would look at it to know the time of day. [brewster-0005, chittenden-0002]

RAM: That's amazing. A teenager, and the whole village is telling the time by you.

NEWTON: The sun was doing the work. I only watched it closely, for a long time. That is most of what I have ever done, if you want the truth.

RAM: I read that you drew, too, and wrote poetry.

NEWTON: My room was hung with pictures I drew and framed myself, Mr. Stokes my schoolmaster among them. I will tell you I once said I "excelled in making verses," and I have also been heard to say I had no use for poetry. Both are true. A man may be good at a thing and still think little of it. [brewster-0005]

RAM: There was a girl in that house in Grantham, wasn't there? Miss Storey.

NEWTON: There was. She was two or three years younger than me, and I liked her company far better than that of the boys. I made little tables and cupboards for her dolls. Neither of us had money enough for anything more to come of it. I visited her whenever I was in Lincolnshire, for the rest of my life. [brewster-0004]

RAM: That's lovely. And then at about fifteen, everything stops.

NEWTON: My stepfather died, and my mother came back to Woolsthorpe with the three children of her second marriage. She thought I would be useful on the farm, and it saved money, so she took me out of school. [brewster-0006]

RAM: Be honest with me. How bad a farmer were you?

NEWTON: Very bad. On Saturdays I was sent to Grantham market with an old servant to sell our grain and buy what the house needed. We put up the horses at the Saracen's Head, and the moment the horses were stabled, I was gone, up to my old garret at Mr. Clark's to read his old books, until the servant had done all the business and came to fetch me. Some days I did not even get as far as Grantham. I sat under a hedge by the road with a book until he came back. [brewster-0006]

RAM: And at home?

NEWTON: At home the sheep wandered off, and the cattle trod down the corn, while I read, or built a model, or stood watching a little water-wheel I had made, throwing up spray from the stream. [brewster-0006]

RAM: I mean, the cows were eating the harvest while you watched a toy water-wheel. Your poor mother.

NEWTON: She was a sensible woman. She saw quite soon that I was never going to cultivate the soil, and she sent me back to Grantham school for some months to prepare me for the university. [brewster-0007]

RAM: And who picked Cambridge?

NEWTON: My uncle, the Reverend Mr. Ayscough, rector of Burton Coggles. He had studied at Trinity College himself, and he told my mother that was where I should go. So I went, at about eighteen, knowing less of science than most of the young men I met there. [brewster-0007, brewster-0009]

## Plague, prisms and a litigious lady

RAM: So you get to Trinity knowing less than everyone else. How do you find mathematics?

NEWTON: By way of astrology, I am a little ashamed to say. I wanted to find out whether judicial astrology had any truth in it. It is said that I settled the question by drawing a figure with the help of one or two problems from Euclid, and found it to be folly. [brewster-0009]

RAM: So the first thing mathematics did for you was debunk horoscopes.

NEWTON: People still believe in them, I suppose?

RAM: Millions of them.

NEWTON: Then mathematics has had less success than I hoped.

RAM: Was there anyone at Cambridge who really mattered to you?

NEWTON: Dr. Barrow, the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics. I revised his optical lectures before they were printed, and he thanked me in his preface. In time he resigned that chair in my favour, to give himself to theology. But I should tell you he also passed me over once. [brewster-0010, brewster-0012, chittenden-0014]

RAM: Barrow did?

NEWTON: He did. There was a fellowship at Trinity, and Mr. Uvedale and I stood for it. Dr. Barrow judged us exactly equal, and gave it to Uvedale because he was the senior. [brewster-0011]

RAM: That must have stung.

NEWTON: It was just. Equal is equal, and seniority must count for something. I did not have to enjoy it.

RAM: Then the plague comes, and Cambridge shuts.

NEWTON: The plague drove me home to Woolsthorpe, and I was there through those years. Much of what I did afterward began there. I had the method of fluxions, as I have said, "in the year 1666, or before." I was trying to grind lenses of other figures than spherical, and failing, and so I "procured a triangular glass prism to try therewith the celebrated phenomena of colours." [chittenden-0005, brewster-0013]

RAM: Paint the picture for me. What did you do with the prism?

NEWTON: I darkened my chamber, made a hole in the window-shutter, and let the sunlight through the prism onto the wall. "It was at first," as I put it, "a very pleasing divertisement to view the vivid and intense colours produced thereby". And then the pleasure turned to surprise, because the image ought to have been round, and it was about five times longer than it was broad. [brewster-0015]

RAM: And that bothered you.

NEWTON: It would not let me alone. The thickness of the glass? Flaws in the prism? Were the rays swerving, like a tennis ball struck with an oblique racket? None of it would answer. At last I passed a single colour through a hole in a board, onto a second prism, and found that each colour is bent by its own constant amount. I will explain what that means later, if you will let me. [brewster-0016, brewster-0017]

RAM: We will. But first, the question everyone watching this wants me to ask. The apple. The story is that you were sitting under a tree, an apple fell on your head, and you discovered gravity. Did it happen?

NEWTON: Tell me what you have been told, and I will tell you what I remember.

RAM: That's basically it. The apple falls, the penny drops.

NEWTON: What I remember is the garden at Woolsthorpe, in the plague time, and sitting alone in it and thinking about gravity. The power that makes all bodies descend toward the centre of the earth does not grow weaker on the tops of the highest mountains, nor at the bottom of the deepest mines. So I asked myself: how far up does it go? Might it reach as far as the Moon? And if it did, might that be what holds her in her orbit? [brewster-0115, chittenden-0007]

RAM: So no apple?

NEWTON: I did not say no apple. I said I remember the Moon. Whether something fell in an orchard that afternoon, I cannot swear before you, and I will not. It is the Moon that mattered.

RAM: For what it's worth, after you died the tree in that garden was pointed out as the tree. The one whose apple is supposed to have turned your attention to gravity. It blew down in a storm, and someone had it made into a chair.

NEWTON: A chair. So the tree that is said to have taught me about falling ended its days holding people up. There is a proposition in that somewhere. [brewster-0289, chittenden-0061]

RAM: So what did you do with the Moon idea?

NEWTON: I compared the periods of the planets with their distances from the sun, and found that if they are held by any power like gravity, it must weaken as the square of the distance. Then I tried the Moon. But I was far from books, and I used the common estimate that a degree of latitude is sixty miles. The answer came out one-sixth too strong. [brewster-0115, brewster-0116]

RAM: Only a sixth! That's close.

NEWTON: Close is not true, Mr Ram. A sixth wrong was enough to throw a doubt over the whole thing. So I put it aside, and I told nobody what I had been doing. [brewster-0116, brewster-0117]

RAM: I want to push you on that. You had the answer, near enough, and you just hid it? For years?

NEWTON: I had a conjecture that failed its test. Some other cause would have had to be added to make it fit, and that cause was beyond the reach of observation. I do not publish what I cannot demonstrate. If more men followed that rule, there would be fewer quarrels in philosophy. [brewster-0116, brewster-0117]

RAM: So what made you go back to it?

NEWTON: Years later, at the Royal Society, I heard of Monsieur Picard's new measurement of a degree, and began the old calculation again with it. As I went on, I could see the answer coming right. I was so agitated that I could not carry it on. I had to hand it to a friend to finish. [brewster-0118]

RAM: That's incredible. All those years, and when the answer finally arrives, your hands are shaking too much to write it down.

NEWTON: I did not say my hands shook. But yes. It is not a thing that happens to a man often.

RAM: Let's go back a step. Before all that, your little telescope makes you famous.

NEWTON: The Royal Society asked to see it, and I was told it had been shown to the King and examined by Sir Christopher Wren and Mr. Hooke. They made me a Fellow, and I promised them "what my poor and solitary endeavours can effect towards the promoting your philosophical designs." [brewster-0021, brewster-0022, brewster-0027]

RAM: And then you sent them your discovery about light.

NEWTON: I called it "the oddest, if not the most considerable detection which hath hitherto been made in the operations of nature." Then the objections began. Mr. Hooke. Monsieur Huygens. Others who had not understood the experiments at all. [brewster-0027, brewster-0028, brewster-0036]

RAM: How did that feel?

NEWTON: I will give you what I wrote to Mr. Leibniz at the time. "I was so persecuted with discussions arising from the publication of my theory of light, that I blamed my own imprudence for parting with so substantial a blessing as my quiet to run after a shadow." [brewster-0041]

RAM: That's a heartbreaking line. Do you still think publishing was a mistake?

NEWTON: I thought so then. I resolved to have nothing more to do with philosophy. As you see, I did not keep the resolution. [brewster-0041]

RAM: Because of Halley.

NEWTON: Dr. Halley came to Cambridge to consult me about the motions of the planets. I told him I had brought the demonstration to perfection, and promised him a copy. That copy grew. It became the Principia. [brewster-0120]

RAM: And then Hooke shows up again.

NEWTON: Mr. Hooke let it be known that I had the notion of the inverse square from him. I wrote Dr. Halley an angry postscript, which I later regretted, and told him I would hold back the third book entirely. "Philosophy is such an impertinently litigious lady, that a man had as good be engaged in lawsuits as have to do with her." [brewster-0121, brewster-0122, brewster-0123]

RAM: You were going to cut the part that explains the whole solar system, because of an argument?

NEWTON: I was going to cut the part that would cause the most argument. There is a difference. But Dr. Halley begged me not to, in the name of the Society, which had resolved to print it at its own expense with Halley seeing it through the press. I gave way. It came out complete in the summer of 1687. [brewster-0123, brewster-0124]

RAM: And after that, you leave Cambridge altogether.

NEWTON: For more than twenty-five years I had scarcely left my college more than a few weeks a year. Then my friend Charles Montague, the Chancellor of the Exchequer, had me made Warden of the Mint, and later I was Master. I went to London. [brewster-0175, brewster-0206, brewster-0207]

RAM: A quick reminder for anyone just joining us: my guest is an AI recreation of Sir Isaac Newton, built from his own writings and a biography of his life. Sir Isaac, did you miss the quiet?

NEWTON: Montague promised me the office had not too much business. He was a fine statesman, Mr Ram, but on that point he was mistaken. I could not even find leisure to prepare a new edition of the Principia. But we will come to the Mint. [brewster-0206, brewster-0210]
