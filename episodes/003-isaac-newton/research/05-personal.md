# Friendship, love and loss

Goal: The guest's inner life: friendships, love, family, grief.
Target: 8 min (~1200 words)

Beats:
- Closest friendships and what made them work.
- Love, marriage or the lack of it; family ties.
- A loss that changed them.

## Questions

1. Your mother remarried when you were three and left you with your grandmother, then later pulled you out of school to run the farm. What was that like? (brewster-0002, -0006, -0007, chittenden-0003)
2. You never married. The biographers say there was a girl at Grantham, Miss Storey. Who was she to you? (brewster-0004, chittenden-0003)
3. Who were your real friends: Barrow, Halley, Montague? Halley seems to have been the one who got the Principia out of you. (brewster-0012, chittenden-0014, principia-0002, brewster-0120 to -0124, brewster-0204 to -0206)
4. Your niece Catherine Barton ran your house in London for twenty years. What was she like to live with? (brewster-0270, brewster-0208, chittenden-0049)
5. The dog Diamond and the candle: did that really happen, and what did you lose? (brewster-0177, -0183, -0184, chittenden-0038)
6. In 1693 you wrote some strange letters to Pepys and Locke, and then apologised. What was going on? (brewster-0185 to -0199)
7. You fought with Hooke, Flamstead and Leibnitz. Do you regret how you behaved in any of those fights? (brewster-0039 to -0041, brewster-0121 to -0124, brewster-0200, brewster-0169 to -0174)
8. People call you a loner. Is that fair? (brewster-0286, -0287, chittenden-0041, brewster-0041)

## Passages

Mother and family
- brewster-0002 (biography, ch. I): after three years his mother married the Rev. Barnabas Smith, rector of North Witham, and "left him under the care of her own mother". At Grantham he was low in the school until a boy kicked him in the stomach; he then worked until he was head boy (Newton told Conduit this).
- brewster-0006, -0007 (biography, ch. I): Smith dies 1656; mother returns to Woolsthorpe with three Smith children (Mary, Benjamin, Hannah); calls Isaac home at about 15 "chiefly from a motive of economy" to run the farm; he slips off to read in the apothecary's garret or under a hedge while sheep stray; she "wisely resolved" to send him back to school; uncle Rev. W. Ayscough recommends Trinity.
- chittenden-0003 (biography): same story; adds that Newton drew and framed pictures, "excelled in making verses" (Mrs. Vincent's recollection).
- brewster-0275 (biography, ch. XVIII): estate of about 32,000l. divided among four nephews and four nieces of the half-blood, grandchildren of his mother by Smith.
- brewster-0288 / chittenden-0061 (biography): Stukeley guesses the Woolsthorpe study was used "perhaps when he visited his mother from the university" (speculation by Stukeley).

Love and never marrying
- brewster-0004 (biography, ch. I): Miss Storey, two or three years younger, lived nearly six years in the same Grantham house; he made "little tables and cupboards" for her and her friends' dolls; "there is reason to believe" friendship rose to "a higher passion" but small fortunes on both sides prevented marriage (Brewster's hedge). She married twice; as Mrs. Vincent, aged 82, told Stukeley about his youth in 1727. He visited her in Lincolnshire and helped her with money all his life.
- chittenden-0003 (biography): "Miss Storey was afterwards twice married; Newton, never"; "it is believed" the friendship became passion.
- brewster-0194 (biography, Newton's letter to Locke, 16 Sept 1693): he had suspected Locke of trying to "embroil me with women". This is his own phrase, written during the 1693 illness.

Friendships
- brewster-0010, -0012, chittenden-0014 (biography): Barrow, "his friend and tutor"; thanked Newton in the preface to his optical lectures for revising them; Newton sent his fluxions treatise (Analysis per Equationes) to Barrow in June 1669, who passed it to Collins; in 1669 Barrow resigned the Lucasian chair "in favour of Newton". NB brewster-0011: Barrow, as Master of Trinity, gave a fellowship to Uvedale over Newton as the senior candidate.
- principia-0002 (primary, Author's Preface): Newton's own thanks to Halley: "it was to his solicitations that its becoming public is owing".
- brewster-0120 (biography, ch. XI): August 1684 Halley goes to Cambridge to ask about the orbit; Newton says he has the demonstration.
- brewster-0123, -0124 (biography, ch. XI): Newton's letter to Halley, 20 June 1686: he plans to "suppress" Book III because of Hooke; "Philosophy is such an impertinently litigious lady ..."; Halley begs him "not to let your resentments run so high"; he gives in.
- brewster-0285 (biography, ch. XIX): when Halley spoke disrespectfully of religion, Newton checked him: "I have studied these things,—you have not." Brewster gives no source; recorded saying.
- brewster-0204 to -0207 (biography, ch. XIV): Charles Montague, 20 years younger, a disciple; tried to found a philosophical society at Cambridge with him in 1684 (failed "from the want ... of persons willing to try experiments", Newton's phrase reported); as Chancellor of the Exchequer made him Warden of the Mint (Montague's letter of 19 March 1695 is Montague's words, not Newton's).
- brewster-0192 (biography, ch. XIII): but in letters to Locke (Jan/Feb 1691-2) Newton wrote that Montague "upon an old grudge ... is false to me, I have done with him"; "My inclinations are to sit still." Newton's own words.
- brewster-0193 to -0199 (biography, ch. XIII): Locke friendship: Newton's apology letter and Locke's generous reply (Locke's words). See quotes.
- brewster-0188 to -0191 (biography, ch. XIII): Pepys's worried letters about Newton; Millington's report. These are the friends' words, not Newton's.
- brewster-0284 / chittenden-0057 (biography): Henry Pemberton (editor of the 3rd edition) on how warmly Newton took his corrections; Pemberton's words, not Newton's.
- brewster-0307 to -0309 (biography, ch. XIX): Conduit's memorandum of a fireside conversation at Kensington, 7 March 1724-5; shows real intimacy: Newton "laughing". Conduit's narration, with a few of Newton's words in single quotes.

Catherine Barton
- brewster-0270 (biography, ch. XVIII): "beautiful and accomplished niece", widow of Colonel Barton, educated at Newton's expense, kept his house for his last twenty years in London, later married John Conduit; the couple lived in his house till his death.
- brewster-0208 (biography, ch. XIV): Halifax (Montague) "conceived a strong attachment" for her after his first wife's death; "she did not escape the censures of her contemporaries" but Brewster calls her "a woman of strict honour and virtue"; "We are not acquainted with the causes which prevented her union" with Halifax; he left her a very large part of his fortune and Newton 100l.
- chittenden-0049 (biography): same, briefly.
- brewster-0275 (biography): their daughter Catharine Conduit received the Kensington estate; Conduit succeeded Newton at the Mint.

Diamond, the fire and the 1692-93 illness
- brewster-0177 (biography, ch. XIII): the Diamond story: winter morning, dog overturns a lighted taper, burns papers on optical experiments; Newton "is said" to have exclaimed (see quotes). **Brewster's doubt:** "It is a curious circumstance that Newton never refers to the experiments which he is said to have lost on this occasion, and his nephew, Mr. Conduit, makes no allusion to the event itself." Brewster wraps it all in "is said", "it has been stated".
- brewster-0178 (biography): source of the "insanity" claim: Huygens's journal (29 May 1694, from "M. Colin, a Scotsman") via Biot; says he lost his "chymical laboratory and several manuscripts" by fire.
- brewster-0183 (biography): Abraham de la Pryme's diary, 3 Feb 1692: a book on light and colours "established upon thousands of experiments" over "twenty years", costing "many hundred of pounds", burnt when a candle was left burning while he was at chapel; "every one thought he would have run mad ... he was not himself for a month after". **Pryme does not mention a dog.**
- brewster-0184 (biography): Brewster's verdict: Pryme's phrase means only that he was very troubled for a month. Dates conflict with Huygens. He wrote the four Bentley letters (10 Dec 1692 to Feb 1693) in the middle of the supposed madness.
- brewster-0185, -0201 (biography): Brewster: bad health from some time in 1692, sleep and appetite affected; in mid-September 1693 kept awake five nights. Still worked: fluxions example for Wallis (1692), halo observations (June 1692), lunar theory with Flamstead (1694).
- brewster-0186 (biography, Newton to Pepys, 13 Sept 1693): Newton's own words: "neither ate nor slept well this twelvemonth"; wants to "see neither you nor the rest of my friends any more".
- brewster-0188 (biography, Pepys to Millington): Pepys fears "a discomposure in head, or mind, or both" (Pepys's words).
- brewster-0189 (biography, Millington to Pepys, 30 Sept 1693): Newton, unprompted, told Millington at Huntingdon he had written "a very odd letter", in "a distemper that much seized his head", and was "very much ashamed"; Millington: "some small degree of melancholy" but understanding untouched (Millington's words).
- brewster-0194 (biography, Newton to Locke, 16 Sept 1693, "At the Bull, in Shoreditch"): the apology (see quotes).
- brewster-0196, -0197 (biography, Locke's reply, 5 Oct 1693): forgives him "freely and fully" (Locke's words).
- brewster-0198 (biography, Newton to Locke, Cambridge, 5 Oct 1693): explains: slept by the fire, epidemic distemper, "not slept an hour a night for a fortnight together, and for five days together not a wink"; cannot remember what he wrote.
- brewster-0199, -0200, -0202 (biography): Brewster concludes he was "in the full possession of his mental powers". **Brewster has a stated motive:** "a sacred duty to the memory of that great man ... and to the interests of Christianity itself" (brewster-0182).
- chittenden-0038 (biography): Chittenden puts the fire in "the winter of 1691-2", papers "nearly consumed", and gives a slightly different wording of the Diamond exclamation (see quotes). chittenden-0039: Chittenden agrees with Brewster that his reason was never "dethroned"; says the shock "may have originated" two years of nervous trouble (speculation).

Feuds
- Hooke (optics): brewster-0039 (biography, ch. IV): Hooke accepted the observations but argued for pulses and only two colours; Newton replied by letter to Oldenburg, 11 June 1672. brewster-0040: Huygens too; Newton: "a little ungrateful to me". brewster-0041: his reaction: he intends "to be no farther solicitous about matters of philosophy"; "persecuted with discussions" (letter to Leibnitz, 9 Dec 1675).
- Hooke (gravity): brewster-0121 (biography, ch. XI): 28 April 1686 Hooke claims the inverse square at the Royal Society and in the coffee-house; Halley's letter of 22 May. brewster-0122: Newton's "angry and satirical postscript" ("the fruit of my own garden"); after Halley's reassurance he regrets it and proposes a scholium crediting Wren, Hooke and Halley. brewster-0123: threatens to suppress Book III.
- Flamstead: brewster-0200 (biography, ch. XIII): visit to Greenwich 1 Sept 1694; Newton's letter of 7 Oct 1694 (quote below); correspondence till 1698; Brewster says Flamstead treated requests "as if they were idle intrusions". chittenden-0039: same visit. brewster-0290: about thirty-four letters to Flamstead kept at Corpus Christi, Oxford. **That is all the indexed sources say about Flamstead** (see Gaps).
- Leibnitz: brewster-0169 to -0174 (biography, ch. XII): Commercium Epistolicum (1713); Bernoulli's charge; Newton's letter to Abbé Conti, 26 Feb 1715-16, written only after George I asked when his answer would appear (brewster-0171); Newton says he withheld two letters favourable to himself from the committee (brewster-0170, reported speech); after Leibnitz's death (14 Nov 1716) the scholium mentioning him was struck from the 3rd edition "either by Newton or by the editor" (brewster-0173). Brewster's judgement, "The conduct of Newton was at all times dignified and just" (brewster-0174), is openly partisan. chittenden-0047: Chittenden says Newton took no part in either edition of the Commercium and "kept aloof" till the King's request.
- brewster-0213, -0214 (biography, ch. XIV): Cotes offered to attack Leibnitz in the 2nd-edition preface; Brewster infers from the preface that Newton "had prohibited any personal reference to the conduct of Leibnitz" (inference, no letter quoted).

Solitude and absent-mindedness
- brewster-0286 (biography, ch. XIX): sat on his bedside for hours after rising without dressing; had to be reminded of meals.
- chittenden-0041 (biography): same; adds that he could break off and later return to "the very point where they ceased".
- brewster-0287 (biography): Hearne: "full of thought, and spoke very little in company"; arm out of each side of the coach. Atterbury: "something rather languid in his look". Conduit: "lively and piercing eye", hair "as white as silver". (Others' words.)
- brewster-0284, chittenden-0049 (biography): the biographers' opposite picture: modest, affable, suits himself to every company.
- brewster-0175 (biography): 1669-1695 "seldom absent from his college more than three or four weeks in the year".

## Exact quotes

All quotes in this section are from biography passages. They are Newton's own words (letters or recorded sayings) as the biographer reproduces them. None are the biographers' sentences.

Friendship and Halley
- "Philosophy is such an impertinently litigious lady, that a man had as good be engaged in lawsuits as have to do with her." (brewster-0123, Newton to Halley, 20 June 1686)
- "I found it so formerly, and now I can no sooner come near her again but she gives me warning." (brewster-0123, same letter)
- "The third I now design to suppress." (brewster-0123, same letter, of Book III)
- "I have studied these things,—you have not." (brewster-0285, recorded saying to Halley; Brewster gives no source)
- "Being fully convinced that Mr. Montague, upon an old grudge which I thought had been worn out, is false to me, I have done with him, and intend to sit still, unless my Lord Monmouth be still my friend." (brewster-0192, Newton to Locke, 26 Jan 1691-2)
- "My inclinations are to sit still." (brewster-0192, Newton to Locke, 16 Feb 1691-2)
- Primary, for Halley: "it was to his solicitations that its becoming public is owing" (principia-0002, Author's Preface)

The 1693 letters
- "I am extremely troubled at the embroilment I am in, and have neither ate nor slept well this twelvemonth, nor have my former consistency of mind." (brewster-0186, Newton to Pepys, 13 Sept 1693)
- "I must withdraw from your acquaintance, and see neither you nor the rest of my friends any more, if I may but leave them quietly." (brewster-0186, same letter)
- "Being of opinion that you endeavoured to embroil me with women, and by other means, I was so much affected with it, as that when one told me you were sickly and would not live, I answered, ’twere better if you were dead. I desire you to forgive me this uncharitableness" (brewster-0194, Newton to Locke, 16 Sept 1693)
- "I am your most humble and unfortunate servant" (brewster-0194, same letter)
- "The last winter, by sleeping too often by my fire, I got an ill habit of sleeping; and a distemper, which this summer has been epidemical, put me farther out of order, so that when I wrote to you, I had not slept an hour a night for a fortnight together, and for five days together not a wink." (brewster-0198, Newton to Locke, 5 Oct 1693)
- "I remember I wrote to you, but what I said of your book I remember not." (brewster-0198, same letter)

Diamond (hearsay, two versions; both biographers present it as reported, not attested)
- "Oh, Diamond, Diamond, little do you know the mischief you have done me!" (brewster-0177; Brewster: "it has been stated that ... he exclaimed")
- "Oh Diamond, Diamond, little knowest thou the mischief thou hast done." (chittenden-0038; Chittenden states it as fact)

Feuds
- "My letter to Hugenius was directed to Mr. Oldenburg, who used to keep the originals. His papers came into Mr. Hooke’s possession. Mr. Hooke, knowing my hand, might have the curiosity to look into that letter, and there take the notion of comparing the forces of the planets arising from their circular motion; and so what he wrote to me afterward about the rate of gravity might be nothing but the fruit of my own garden." (brewster-0122, Newton to Halley, postscript, 1686)
- "As for Mr. Huygens’s expression, I confess it was a little ungrateful to me, to meet with objections which had been answered before, without having the least reason given me why those answers were insufficient." (brewster-0040)
- "I was so persecuted with discussions arising from the publication of my theory of light, that I blamed my own imprudence for parting with so substantial a blessing as my quiet to run after a shadow." (brewster-0041, Newton to Leibnitz, 9 Dec 1675)
- "the moon’s theory may be reduced to a good degree of exactness, perhaps to the exactness of two or three minutes." (brewster-0200, Newton to Flamstead, 7 Oct 1694)
- "But as he has lately attacked me with an accusation which amounts to plagiary; if he goes on to accuse me, it lies upon him by the laws of all nations to prove his accusations, on pain of being accounted guilty of calumny." (brewster-0171, Newton to Abbé Conti, 26 Feb 1715-16)
- "But he is the aggressor, and it lies upon him to prove the charge." (brewster-0171, same letter)
- "and I am not ashamed of it." (brewster-0171, same letter, of a paragraph admitting his ignorance)

Conduit fireside (Newton's words in Conduit's memorandum)
- "I do not deal in conjectures." (brewster-0309)
- "that concerned us more;" (brewster-0309; Conduit adds that Newton, laughing, said "that he had said enough for people to know his meaning." That second part is in indirect speech.)

## Gaps

- **Mother's death and his feelings for her.** No indexed passage covers her death, any last illness, or any letter between them. Her first name is not given in my passages. The sources give only the remarriage, the grandmother, the farm episode and her decision to send him back to school. The guest should not invent grief or resentment. If asked, he can say little.
- **His father** appears only as a name in the 1798 tablet (brewster-0289). There is nothing on growing up without him.
- **Catherine Barton.** Covered only as housekeeper, niece, Halifax's beneficiary and Conduit's wife. **No letters between her and Newton, and no anecdotes.** Brewster mentions "censures of her contemporaries" (about Halifax) but rejects them. Do not speculate further.
- **Never marrying.** Beyond Miss Storey (hedged: "there is reason to believe", "it is believed") the sources give no statement from Newton on marriage or celibacy. Do not invent one.
- **Diamond.** Brewster doubts it. Newton never mentions the lost experiments. Conduit is silent. Pryme's diary (the earliest English account) has a candle but **no dog**. Brewster calls the "derangement" a misrepresentation, and he has an openly religious motive. Chittenden simply repeats the story as fact. The guest can say he remembers losing papers to a candle, but should hedge the dog story or treat it as a tale others tell.
- **Flamstead feud.** The indexed sources are thin: the 1694 visit, one quoted line, the claim that Flamstead treated his requests as "idle intrusions", and the count of surviving letters. There is **nothing on the printing of Flamstead's catalogue, Prince George, the burning of copies, or Newton's use of his Royal Society presidency against Flamstead.** (A search for "Flamsteed" returns no hits; the texts use "Flamstead".) The guest must not invent details.
- **Hooke beyond the 1672 and 1686 rows.** Nothing on their later relations or on Hooke's death. The "shoulders of giants" letter is confirmed absent (no hit for "shoulders" or "giants").
- **Leibnitz.** Brewster and Chittenden are strongly pro-Newton. Brewster says the 1722 "general review" was "falsely ascribed to Newton" (brewster-0173). Modern viewers may have heard that Newton secretly wrote the Royal Society report and its review. That claim is **not in our sources**, so the host may raise it only as something "historians say", flagged for checking. The guest may give his side as recorded (he says he did not interfere; brewster-0170).
- **Alchemy.** Contrary to the briefing, there is a little: brewster-0254 reports William Law's claim that Newton and "one Dr. Newton, his relation" set up furnaces "in quest of the philosopher's tincture" for several months. It also mentions Newton's transcripts of Flamel among the Portsmouth papers. Brewster says "there is no reason to suppose that Sir Isaac Newton was a believer in the doctrines of alchymy". Also brewster-0178 (Huygens's note) mentions the fire destroying his "chymical laboratory". Thin, second-hand and hedged: one cautious line at most.
- **Halley's paying for the Principia.** The sources say the Society resolved to print it "at the society's expense" with Halley supervising (brewster-0123). That Halley paid out of his own pocket is **not** in our sources.
