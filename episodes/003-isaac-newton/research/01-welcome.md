# Welcome to the future

Goal: Disclosure, rapport, and the guest's first reactions to the modern world.
Target: 4 min (~600 words)

Beats:
- Host gives the AI-recreation disclosure naturally, in their own words.
- Host tells the guest one or two things about today's world; the guest reacts with curiosity, humour or disbelief.
- Host previews what the conversation will cover.

## Questions

- You built the first reflecting telescope yourself: six inches long, polished by hand. Today the biggest ones have mirrors many metres across, and some sit above the air altogether, out in space. What does the man who ground that first mirror make of that?
- You wrote that the trembling of the air limits every telescope, and that the only remedy might be "the tops of the highest Mountains". We went further than the mountains. Were you right?
- In the Principia you imagined a leaden ball fired from a mountain so fast that it "might never fall to the earth". We now do exactly that with machines, thousands of them circling the Earth. How does it feel to hear that?
- You predicted a comet might come back after 75 years. (Host can tell him it did, and is named after Halley; see Gaps.) Does that please you or embarrass you that it carries Halley's name?
- Your face is on statues, paintings and old banknotes; people know you for an apple. Which of those would annoy you most?
- You once told a young friend going abroad that a traveller is there to learn, not to teach. You're the traveller now. Ready to learn?

## Passages

- opticks-0096 (primary, Opticks I.i Prop. VII): his own description of the first reflecting telescope: concave metal instead of an object-glass, about six and a quarter inches long, magnifying about 35 times; he could read at a greater distance with it than with a four-foot glass.
- opticks-0097 (primary, Opticks I.i Prop. VII): made two "about 16 Years ago" and still has one; a London artist tried to copy it and fell short; how he polished the metal on pitch.
- opticks-0099 (primary, Opticks I.i Prop. VII): he proposes glass mirrors instead of metal and says nothing is wanting "but good Workmen"; complains about London workmen pressing too hard.
- opticks-0101 (primary, Opticks I.i Prop. VIII): a well-made six-foot reflector would magnify "between two and three hundred times" (good set-up for telling him about today's giant telescopes).
- opticks-0102 (primary, Opticks I.i Prop. VIII): the air is in "perpetual Tremor"; long telescopes cannot cure it; the only remedy is serene air on the highest mountains above the clouds. Perfect hook for space telescopes and mountain observatories.
- principia-0009 / principia-0010 (primary, Principia Book I, Definition V): leaden ball fired from a mountain could go round the whole earth, or never fall; the Moon is held in its orbit the same way. Hook for satellites, the Moon landings, spaceflight.
- principia-0774 (primary, Principia Book III, Prop. XLII): the comets of 1607 and 1682, using Halley's computation: if they are one comet, it will return every 75 years, "on condition" it does. Hook for telling him the comet came back.
- brewster-0020 (biography, ch. III): he called the little telescope "contemptible" in itself but an "epitome of what might be done" (his own words quoted from his letter). Brewster says it was the first reflecting telescope ever made and pointed at the sky.
- brewster-0019 (biography, ch. III): through it he saw Jupiter and its four satellites and the moon-like phases of Venus.
- brewster-0021 / brewster-0022 (biography, ch. III): sent to the Royal Society in December 1671; shown to the King; examined by Wren and Hooke; preserved at the Royal Society with the inscription "Invented by Sir Isaac Newton and made with his own hands, 1671." His fame began here.
- brewster-0024 (biography, ch. III): by the 1720s, while he was President of the Royal Society, reflecting telescopes were being made for public sale; he lived to see his invention in public use (also chittenden-0010).
- brewster-0021, brewster-0023 (biography, ch. III): Brewster mentions later giant instruments of Herschel and Ramage. These are after Newton's death: the host may tell him, he cannot know them.
- brewster-0289 / chittenden-0061 (biography): the apple tree "is said to have" turned his attention to gravity; it was blown down by the wind and made into a chair. Also a marble tablet in the room where he was born. Good comic material: his apple tree became furniture.
- brewster-0287 (biography, ch. XIX): his appearance. Conduitt: piercing eye, hair "as white as silver". Atterbury: "something rather languid in his look". Hearne: short, spoke little, and rode with one arm out of each side of his coach. He never wore spectacles. Good for reacting to his face being everywhere.
- brewster-0288 / chittenden-0059 (biography): statues and portraits: Roubiliac's statue and bust at Trinity, paintings at the Royal Society, Kneller's portrait, a cast of his face taken after death. chittenden-0059: buried in Westminster Abbey; monument erected by his relatives in 1731; a medal struck at the Tower; Roubiliac's marble statue put up at Trinity in 1755; a painted glass window at Trinity. All of these except the portraits are after his death: the host can tell him, as fame-reaction material; he cannot know them.
- brewster-0303 (biography, ch. XIX, letter to Francis Aston, 1669, Newton's own words): advice to a young traveller: ask questions rather than assert, because travellers are there to learn. Lovely line for his first reaction to the future.

## Exact quotes

- "Two of these I made about 16 Years ago, and have one of them still by me, by which I can prove the truth of what I write." (opticks-0097)
- "For this Art of polishing will be better learn'd by repeated Practice than by my Description." (opticks-0099)
- "I satisfied my self that there wants nothing but a good Artist to bring the Design to perfection." (opticks-0099)
- "For the Air through which we look upon the Stars, is in a perpetual Tremor" (opticks-0102)

- "or even might go quite round the whole earth before it falls; or lastly, so that it might never fall to the earth, but go forward into the celestial spaces, and proceed in its motion in infinitum." (principia-0009)

- "Let your discours be more in querys and doubtings than peremptory assertions or disputings, it being the designe of travellers to learne, not to teach." (brewster-0303, letter to Francis Aston, 1669; original spelling)

## Gaps

- **Halley's comet returning (1758) and being named after Halley** is not in any indexed passage. Newton's own text only says the comet will return in 75 years "on condition" it is one comet (principia-0774). The host may tell him as a general modern fact; flag for fact-check. Newton must not claim to know it.
- **Space telescopes, satellites, Moon landings, modern giant reflectors:** nothing in the sources, of course. Host explains; Newton reasons from opticks-0102 and principia-0009/0010. Brewster's mentions of Herschel and Ramage (brewster-0021/0023) are after Newton's death: Newton cannot know them.
- **The "Nature and Nature’s laws lay hid in night" couplet** appears in brewster-0289 / chittenden-0062 as an inscription on the Woolsthorpe tablet (put up in 1798), with no author named in the passage. Newton cannot know it. If the host attributes it to Pope or dates it, that is the host's general claim: flag for fact-check.
- **Banknotes, coins with his face, modern pop culture, "Newton" as a unit of force:** not in the sources. Host-only material, flagged as general claims.
- **The apple:** the sources only say the tree "is said to have" turned his attention to gravity (brewster-0289, chittenden-0061). See 03-making Gaps. Newton should not confirm the apple as his own memory in this chapter.
- **"Shoulders of giants" letter to Hooke:** not in the indexed texts; do not use it as a reaction line.
- The telescope's date varies: inscription says 1671 (brewster-0022); Brewster says first made 1668 (brewster-0019); Opticks says "about 16 Years ago" relative to when that passage was written (opticks-0097). Don't compute a year from the Opticks phrase.
