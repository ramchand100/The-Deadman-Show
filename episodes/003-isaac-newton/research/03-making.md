# Becoming who they were

Goal: The career path: lucky breaks, setbacks, the people met along the way, and how the major work got made.
Target: 8 min (~1200 words)

Beats:
- First jobs and how the guest got them.
- Travel, famous people met, and what they were really like.
- How the great work was written: years, doubts, habits.

## Questions

- You got into mathematics at Cambridge through astrology, of all things. What happened?
- The plague closes Cambridge and sends you home to Woolsthorpe. In about two years you have fluxions, the colours, and the start of gravity. What was a day like there?
- So: the apple. Our viewers want to know. Did it really happen?
- Your first try at the Moon came out wrong, about a sixth too strong, and you put it away for years. Why didn't you tell anyone?
- Barrow gives up his professorship to you at 26 or so. Why? And what did you owe him?
- Your little telescope reaches the King and makes you famous, and then your paper on light brings Hooke, Huygens and the rest down on you. You wrote that you had given up your quiet "to run after a shadow". Was publishing a mistake?
- 1684: Halley turns up in Cambridge with a question. How did that visit become the Principia, and why did you nearly cut the third book?
- Then you leave Cambridge for the Mint, the Royal Society and a knighthood. Did you miss the quiet life?

## Passages

Cambridge and Barrow
- brewster-0009 (biography, ch. II): admitted to Trinity (5 June 1660 per Brewster; see Gaps); turned to mathematics wanting to test judicial astrology, and is "said to have" exposed its folly using one or two of Euclid's problems; skipped Euclid as self-evident and mastered Descartes's Geometry alone; later regretted it (told Pemberton); studied Wallis's Arithmetic of Infinites, Saunderson's Logic, Kepler's Optics, writing comments; "is reported" to have outrun his tutor. Also chittenden-0004.
- brewster-0010 (biography, ch. II): bought a prism in 1664 (from his account of expenses); Barrow, "His friend and tutor", Lucasian Professor from 1663; Newton revised Barrow's optical lectures (published 1669) and is thanked in the preface. Brewster argues Newton had not yet found the composition of light.
- brewster-0011 (biography, ch. II): Barrow's own odd theory of colours (blue sea from salt, etc.); a setback: Newton and Robert Uvedale competed for a fellowship, Barrow judged them equal and gave it to Uvedale as senior.
- brewster-0012 (biography, ch. II): the timeline: sub-sizer 1661, scholar 1664, B.A. 1665, retired to Woolsthorpe 1666 because of plague, Junior Fellow 1667, M.A. and Senior Fellow 1668, and in 1669 Barrow, turning to theology, resigned the Lucasian chair "in favour of Newton". Also chittenden-0010, chittenden-0014.
- chittenden-0005 (biography): Barrow his "early and fast friend"; binomial theorem; fluxions and fluents explained; Newton's own phrase that he had fluxions "in the year 1666, or before."
- chittenden-0014 (biography): June 1669 he gave his tract Analysis per Equationes Numero Terminorum Infinitas to Barrow, who sent it to Collins, who spread it among mathematicians in several countries; lectured on optics 1669-71 as Lucasian Professor.

The plague years at Woolsthorpe
- brewster-0013 (biography, ch. III): 1666: different refrangibility of light established; germ of gravity "in the same year, or at least in 1667"; fluxions "in the year 1666 or before" (Newton's words); he "procured a triangular glass prism to try therewith the celebrated phenomena of colours" (his words); he had been trying to grind non-spherical lenses.
- brewster-0015 to brewster-0018 (biography, ch. III): the darkened room experiment in Newton's own reported words: hole in the window shutter, spectrum five times longer than broad, "a very pleasing divertisement"; ruling out thickness of glass, irregularities, the sun's size, curved paths (he remembered a tennis ball struck with an oblique racket); the crucial experiment with two boards and a second prism; conclusion: light made of rays of different refrangibility; so lenses cannot be perfected and he turned to mirrors. (The physics is chapter 04 territory; use just the room and the tennis ball here.)
- brewster-0019 (biography, ch. III): forced to quit Cambridge by the plague in 1666; more than two years passed before he resumed optics in 1668.
- chittenden-0006 / chittenden-0007 (biography): left Cambridge because of the plague; at Woolsthorpe "in serene contemplation"; "Towards the close of a pleasant day in the early autumn of 1666, he was seated alone beneath a tree, in his garden, absorbed in meditation." (Chittenden's scene-setting; no apple falls in it.)
- brewster-0115 (biography, ch. XI): 1666, sitting alone in the garden at Woolsthorpe thinking about gravity: since it does not weaken on mountains or in mines, might it reach the Moon? Compared the planets' periods and distances: the force must fall off as the square of distance. No apple mentioned. Also chittenden-0008.
- brewster-0116 / brewster-0117 (biography, ch. XI): away from books, he used the common estimate of sixty miles to a degree; the Moon came out one-sixth wrong; he dropped the inquiry and hid it from his friends. chittenden-0009 adds that he guessed some other cause, "perhaps" like Descartes's vortices (Chittenden's speculation).
- brewster-0289 / chittenden-0061 (biography): the apple tree, "the fall of one of the apples of which is said to have turned the attention of Newton to the subject of gravity", was blown down by wind and made into a chair. This is the only apple material in the sources.

Telescope and Royal Society
- brewster-0019 / brewster-0020 (biography, ch. III): 1668, first reflecting telescope, made with his own hands: six inches long, magnified about forty times; saw Jupiter's four satellites and the phases of Venus; letter of 23 February 1668-9; he called it contemptible in itself but an "epitome of what might be done". Also chittenden-0010.
- brewster-0021 / brewster-0022 (biography, ch. III): a second, better telescope; sent to Oldenburg in December 1671; on 11 January 1672 the Society heard it had been shown to the King and examined by Moray, Neale, Wren and Hooke; they sent a description to Huygens to secure his claim. "from this epoch his name began to acquire that celebrity".
- brewster-0027 (biography, ch. IV): proposed for the Royal Society by Seth Ward, Bishop of Salisbury, 23 Dec 1671; elected 11 Jan 1672; his letters to Oldenburg (grateful; "poor and solitary endeavours"; the "oddest, if not the most considerable detection").
- brewster-0028 (biography, ch. IV): the paper on light, letter of 6 February 1672; "solemn thanks"; Ward, Boyle and Hooke asked to report on it; Newton's letter glad to address "so judicious and impartial an assembly" rather than "a prejudiced and common multitude". Also chittenden-0014.
- brewster-0031 (biography, ch. IV): the painter's-powder experiment and the friend stopped at the door asked "which of the two whites were the best" (Newton's own account). Nice story, optional.

The disputes over light
- brewster-0036 (biography, ch. IV): disputes with the Dutch/Flemish objectors (Lucas etc.); Brewster says it cost him more trouble to find their blunders than to establish the truths.
- brewster-0037 / brewster-0038 (biography, ch. IV): Brewster's (hostile) portrait of Hooke: seven years older, an original member, brilliant, inventive, but ill, peevish and greedy for reputation; criticised the telescope "with undue severity" and claimed his own method of perfecting all optical instruments.
- brewster-0039 (biography, ch. IV): Hooke's wave theory and claim of only two colours; Newton's reply of 11 June 1672; no answer came. (Rivals chapter may use more.)
- brewster-0040 (biography, ch. IV): Huygens's objections (white from yellow and blue); Huygens said Newton "maintained his doctrine with some concern"; Newton's reply (quote below).
- brewster-0041 (biography, ch. IV): the effect on him: resolved to be "no farther solicitous about matters of philosophy"; letters of 1675, including to Leibniz, about losing his quiet.

Halley and the Principia
- brewster-0117 / brewster-0118 (biography, ch. XI): 1679 correspondence with Hooke (then Secretary) about a falling body; Newton wrongly said a spiral, Hooke corrected him; this led Newton to the theorem on the ellipse (Newton's own account in his letter to Halley). June 1682: at the Royal Society he heard of Picard's measurement of a degree, redid the Moon calculation, and was so agitated as the answer came right that he could not finish and gave it to a friend. (The friend is unnamed.)
- brewster-0119 (biography, ch. XI): propositions sent to London late 1683; January 1683-4: Halley, Wren and Hooke at a meeting; Hooke claimed he had demonstrated all celestial motions but would conceal it; Wren offered a book worth forty shillings for a proof within two months; Hooke never produced it ("every reason to believe" — Brewster's inference). Also chittenden-0024.
- brewster-0120 (biography, ch. XI): August 1684 Halley went to Cambridge to consult Newton, who said he had perfected the demonstration and promised a copy; November copy received; 10 December Halley reported "De Motu Corporum"; Newton's letter about fitting his notions "suddenly for the press"; a five or six weeks' trip to Lincolnshire interrupted; manuscript sent before end of April.
- brewster-0121 (biography, ch. XI): 28 April 1686 the Principia manuscript, dedicated to the Society, presented; "no more to be added"; Hooke took offence, quarrelled with Hoskins, and claimed in the coffee-house that he gave Newton the first hint; Halley's letter of 22 May telling Newton that Hooke expected mention in the preface.
- brewster-0122 (biography, ch. XI): Newton's angry postscript ("the fruit of my own garden"); Halley says Hooke's claim was exaggerated to him; Newton regrets the postscript and adds a scholium naming Wren, Hooke and Halley. Also chittenden-0026.
- brewster-0123 (biography, ch. XI): council resolved 19 May 1686 to print at the Society's expense with Halley superintending; Newton's letter of 20 June: plan of three books, two months of failed comet calculations, and the threat to suppress the third book ("impertinently litigious lady").
- brewster-0124 (biography, ch. XI): Halley begs him not to suppress Book III; Book II presented 2 March 1686-7, Book III 6 April, published May 1687.

The Mint, the Royal Society, the knighthood
- brewster-0203 (biography, ch. XIV): at 53 he still had only his professor's salary and small rents; Brewster complains England gave him nothing (Brewster's opinion).
- brewster-0204 (biography, ch. XIV): Charles Montague, twenty years younger, his friend from Cambridge; in 1684 they tried to found a philosophical society at Cambridge that failed for want, as Newton put it, of people willing to try experiments; both sat in the Convention Parliament; Montague Chancellor of the Exchequer in 1694; the coin was debased and he planned a recoinage against opposition.
- brewster-0205 / brewster-0206 (biography, ch. XIV): Montague consulted Newton, Locke and Halley on the recoinage; his letter of 19 March 1695 offering the wardenship: worth five or six hundred pounds a year, "has not too much business", come and kiss the King's hand. (Montague's words, not Newton's.)
- brewster-0207 (biography, ch. XIV): his mathematical and chemical knowledge useful; recoinage done in two years; Master of the Mint 1699, worth 1200-1500l. a year, held for life; wrote an official report on the coinage and a table of assays of foreign coins; kept his professorship as Warden; made Whiston his deputy in 1699 and resigned the chair in 1703.
- brewster-0209 (biography, ch. XIV): foreign associate of the Paris Academy of Sciences (1699 charter); MP for Cambridge University again in 1701; President of the Royal Society 1703, re-elected every year for the rest of his life; knighted 16 April 1705 by Queen Anne at Trinity Lodge (Bentley's residence) during a royal visit from Newmarket, along with the vice-chancellor John Ellis and James Montague; then lost the 1705 university election "by a very great majority" (Brewster offers two guesses why).
- brewster-0210 (biography, ch. XIV): Mint business kept him from a second edition of the Principia; Bentley got Roger Cotes to edit it (1709).
- brewster-0175 (biography, ch. XIII): from 1669 to 1695 he "seems to have been" seldom away from his college more than three or four weeks a year (Brewster hedges; useful for the travel beat: he barely travelled).
- brewster-0285 (biography, ch. XIX): in London he lived handsomely, kept a carriage and three male and three female servants; frugal diet, simple dress.

Habits
- brewster-0277 / chittenden-0041 (biography): he said his discoveries came not from any extraordinary sagacity but "solely to industry and patient thought" (reported speech); kept a subject constantly before him; sat on his bed for hours half-dressed, forgot meals (also brewster-0286).

## Exact quotes

All of the following are Newton's own words as reproduced by Brewster or Chittenden (letters or recorded sayings). None are the biographer's sentences.

- "in the year 1666, or before." (chittenden-0005, on having the method of fluxions; brewster-0013 has "in the year 1666 or before")
- "procured a triangular glass prism to try therewith the celebrated phenomena of colours." (brewster-0013; also chittenden-0006)
- "It was at first," says our author, "a very pleasing divertisement to view the vivid and intense colours produced thereby," (brewster-0015; quote only the inner words: "It was at first" ... "a very pleasing divertisement to view the vivid and intense colours produced thereby")
- "I asked him which of the two whites were the best, and wherein they differed!" (brewster-0031, his account of the friend at the door)
- "I am very sensible of the honour done me by the Bishop of Sarum in proposing me a candidate" (brewster-0027, letter to Oldenburg, 6 Jan 1672)
- "communicating what my poor and solitary endeavours can effect towards the promoting your philosophical designs." (brewster-0027)
- "being in my judgment the oddest, if not the most considerable detection which hath hitherto been made in the operations of nature." (brewster-0027, letter of 18 Jan 1671-2 announcing the light discovery)
- "instead of exposing discourses to a prejudiced and common multitude, (by which means many truths have been baffled and lost), I may with freedom apply myself to so judicious and impartial an assembly." (brewster-0028)
- "As for Mr. Huygens’s expression, I confess it was a little ungrateful to me, to meet with objections which had been answered before, without having the least reason given me why those answers were insufficient." (brewster-0040; note the curly apostrophe in Huygens’s)
- "I intend," says he, "to be no farther solicitous about matters of philosophy." (brewster-0041; quote the inner words "I intend" / "to be no farther solicitous about matters of philosophy.")
- "I had some thoughts of writing a further discourse about colours, to be read at one of your assemblies; but find it yet against the grain to put pen to paper any more on that subject;" (brewster-0041, letter of 1675)
- "I was so persecuted with discussions arising from the publication of my theory of light, that I blamed my own imprudence for parting with so substantial a blessing as my quiet to run after a shadow." (brewster-0041, letter to Leibniz, 9 Dec 1675)
- "Mr. Hooke, knowing my hand, might have the curiosity to look into that letter, and there take the notion of comparing the forces of the planets arising from their circular motion; and so what he wrote to me afterward about the rate of gravity might be nothing but the fruit of my own garden." (brewster-0122, angry postscript to Halley, June 1686; he later regretted it)
- "In autumn last I spent two months in calculation to no purpose for want of a good method" (brewster-0123, letter to Halley, 20 June 1686)
- "The third I now design to suppress. Philosophy is such an impertinently litigious lady, that a man had as good be engaged in lawsuits as have to do with her. I found it so formerly, and now I can no sooner come near her again but she gives me warning." (brewster-0123)
- "It will help the sale of the book, which I ought not to diminish now ’tis yours." (brewster-0123; curly apostrophe in ’tis)
- "to enter in the register his notions about motion, and his intentions to fit them suddenly for the press." (brewster-0120; this is in third person as recorded by the secretary, so it cannot be quoted as first-person speech)

Others' words he may recall (not quotable as his own):
- Halley: "I must again beg you," ... "not to let your resentments run so high as to deprive us of your third book" (brewster-0124)
- Montague: "The office is the most proper for you." and "has not too much business to require more attendance than you can spare." (brewster-0206)
- Halley reporting Hooke: "He says you had the notion from him, though he owns the demonstration of the curves generated thereby to be wholly your own." (brewster-0121)

## Gaps

- **The apple:** the sources do NOT narrate an apple falling. Brewster (brewster-0115) and Chittenden (chittenden-0007/0008) put him sitting alone in the Woolsthorpe garden (Chittenden: "beneath a tree") in 1666 thinking about gravity, with no apple. The apple only appears as a tradition about the tree: "is said to have turned the attention of Newton to the subject of gravity" (brewster-0289, chittenden-0061), and the tree was later blown down and made into a chair. Stukeley's or Voltaire's apple accounts are not in our texts. Newton should be guarded: he remembers sitting in the garden thinking about whether gravity reaches the Moon; he should not tell the apple story as his own memory. (An honest line along the lines of: people say an apple, what I remember is the Moon, fits the sources.)
- **"Annus mirabilis" / "prime of my age for invention" memorandum:** not in our sources. Do not use.
- **Plague dates conflict:** Brewster says he left Cambridge in 1666 (brewster-0012, brewster-0019) but also calls the Moon computation "his calculation of 1665" (brewster-0118). Chittenden says 1666. Say the plague years rather than a precise departure date.
- **Trinity admission:** 5 June 1660 (brewster-0009, chittenden-0003) vs. sub-sizer 1661 (brewster-0012). Avoid the year.
- **Why Barrow resigned for him:** only that Barrow resolved to devote himself to theology and resigned "in favour of Newton" (brewster-0012, chittenden-0014). Nothing on Barrow's personal motives or private dealings; no Barrow quotes about Newton beyond his preface acknowledgment (paraphrased in brewster-0010).
- **Halley paying for the Principia:** the sources say the Royal Society resolved to print it "at the society’s expense" with Halley superintending (brewster-0123, chittenden-0024). The modern story that Halley paid out of his own pocket is NOT in our texts. Don't claim it.
- **What Halley actually asked in August 1684** (the famous exchange in which Halley asks what curve and Newton answers an ellipse) is not in our sources: brewster-0120 only says Halley went to consult him and Newton said he had perfected the demonstration. Don't invent the dialogue.
- **The friend who finished the 1682 calculation** is unnamed (brewster-0118).
- **"Shoulders of giants" letter to Hooke (1676)** is not in the indexed texts.
- **Alchemy:** not covered here; Brewster has a later chapter on his "supposed Attachment to Alchymy" (brewster-0249 heading) that I did not research for this chapter. Do not invent experiments or furnaces.
- **Mint prosecutions of counterfeiters/coiners, William Chaloner, the Tower:** not in our texts. The Mint material is limited to: appointment via Montague, the recoinage done in two years, Master from 1699, salary, the report on coinage and assay table (brewster-0205 to 0207). Don't invent his daily Mint work.
- **Mint appointment date:** Montague's letter is dated 19 March 1695 (brewster-0205), Brewster's chapter heading says Warden 1695, the figure file says 1696 (the letter may be Old Style). Have him say when Montague made him Warden, without a year, or flag for the fact-checker.
- **Travel:** the sources show he hardly travelled (seldom away from Cambridge more than three or four weeks a year, brewster-0175); visits to Lincolnshire and London only. Nothing on foreign travel. His travel advice to Francis Aston (brewster-0303) is in 01-welcome.
- **Famous people met:** the sources give names and short scenes (King shown the telescope, but Newton was not said to be present; Wren, Hooke, Halley, Montague, Queen Anne at the knighting, Bentley, Cotes, Locke). No physical descriptions or private conversations with the King or Queen. Don't invent what Queen Anne said.
- Brewster is openly hostile to Hooke and admiring of Newton; his characterisation of Hooke (peevish, "inordinate love of reputation", brewster-0037) is the biographer's opinion, not Newton's words. Newton's own sharpest words about Hooke here are the "fruit of my own garden" postscript, which Brewster says he later regretted (brewster-0122).
