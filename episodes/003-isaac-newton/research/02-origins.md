# Where it all began

Goal: Childhood, family and formative years, told as stories.
Target: 8 min (~1200 words)

Beats:
- Family background and early losses.
- Childhood memories and odd incidents.
- Education, teachers who mattered, and what the guest hated.

## Questions

- You were born on Christmas Day 1642, after your father had already died, and so small nobody expected you to live. What did your family tell you about that night?
- Your mother remarried when you were three and left you with your grandmother. How did a small boy make sense of that?
- At Grantham you were near the bottom of the school, until a boy kicked you in the stomach. What happened next?
- Tell us about the windmill with a mouse inside it. And the kites with lanterns that made the villagers think comets were coming.
- Your water clock and your sundials: the people in the village actually told the time by "Isaac’s dial". How did you make it?
- Your mother brought you home at fifteen to run the farm. How bad a farmer were you, honestly?
- Miss Storey, at the apothecary's house in Grantham: was she your first love?
- Who decided you should go to Cambridge, and what did you take with you?

## Passages

- brewster-0001 (biography, ch. I): born at Woolsthorpe, parish of Colsterworth, about six miles south of Grantham, 25 December 1642 (Old Style), baptised 1 January; father died aged 36 a few months after marrying, before Isaac was born; he was premature and so tiny that two women sent to Lady Pakenham's at North Witham for medicine did not expect to find him alive on their return. Manor worth only 30l. a year; with the Sewstern estate about 80l. Brewster says "it is probable" the farm added to the income (speculation).
- chittenden-0001 (biography): same family background: grandfather Robert Newton is as far back as the line can be traced; two family traditions (Scottish origin, or from Newton in Lancashire); widow lived on about 80 pounds a year. Mother nursed him three years, then remarried and left him with her own mother. Day schools at Skillington and Stoke; Grantham at twelve; inattentive and low in the school until the kick.
- brewster-0002 (biography, ch. I): mother married the Rev. Barnabas Smith, rector of North Witham, about a mile from Woolsthorpe, and left Isaac with her own mother; day schools at Skillington and Stoke; at twelve to Grantham school under Mr. Stokes, boarding with Mr. Clark the apothecary. **The kick story comes from Newton himself, via Conduitt:** he was low in the school, the boy above him kicked him hard in the stomach, he worked until he passed him and then rose to head boy. In playtime he built mechanical things.
- brewster-0003 (biography, ch. I): his tools (little saws, hatchets, hammers); the windmill model copied from one being built near Grantham on the Gunnerby road, set on the roof of his lodging; the mouse called "the miller" on a treadwheel (Brewster gives two versions of how the mouse was driven: a string on its tail, or corn placed above the wheel); the water clock about four feet high, made from a box from Mrs. Clark's brother, filled every morning and used by the Clark family long after he left; a four-wheeled carriage cranked by the person sitting in it, only usable on a floor; he introduced paper kites at school, worked out their best shape and where to attach the string; paper lanterns to walk to school on winter mornings, tied to kites at night so the country people thought they were comets.
- chittenden-0002 (biography): same stories as brewster-0003 (kites, lanterns, windmill and mouse, carriage, water clock, "Isaac's dial"), plus that he was the leader of his fellows. Chittenden says the lanterns delighted his companions.
- brewster-0004 (biography, ch. I): Miss Storey, sister of Dr. Storey of Buckminster, two or three years younger, lived in the same house almost six years; he made little tables and cupboards for her dolls; "there is reason to believe" their friendship became love (biographer's inference), but neither had money enough. She married twice (as Mrs. Vincent); Stukeley visited her in 1727 when she was 82 and got much of the early history from her; Newton visited her and helped her with money all his life. (Also chittenden-0003: "it is believed".) Overlaps with the personal chapter; use lightly here.
- brewster-0005 (biography, ch. I): drawing: his room hung with pictures he drew, coloured and framed, including Dr. Donne, Mr. Stokes the schoolmaster and King Charles I; verses under the King's picture that Mrs. Vincent believed were his (attribution not certain). He told Conduitt "with some expression of pleasure" that he "excelled in making verses", though he was also heard to express contempt for poetry. Sundials: marked hours and half-hours with pins in the yard; "Isaac’s dial" was used by the country people. Brewster says the imperfections of the water clock "had probably" turned him to the sun (speculation).
- brewster-0289 (biography, ch. XIX): the Woolsthorpe house in Brewster's day still contained two of Newton's dials, with the styles missing.
- brewster-0288 / chittenden-0061 (biography): Stukeley's 1721 visit to Woolsthorpe: stone house, his study upstairs with shelves he made himself from deal boxes. (Stukeley's words, not Newton's.)
- brewster-0006 (biography, ch. I): stepfather Smith died 1656; mother returned to Woolsthorpe with three children, Mary, Benjamin and Hannah Smith; she took Isaac (about fifteen) out of school "chiefly from a motive of economy" to help run the farm. Saturdays at Grantham market with an old servant; inn the Saracen's Head in West Gate; he slipped off to Mr. Clark's garret to read old books, or sat reading under a hedge until the servant came back. On the farm, sheep strayed and cattle trampled the corn while he read, built models, or watched a waterwheel he had made. Also chittenden-0003.
- brewster-0007 (biography, ch. I): mother saw he would never farm and sent him back to Grantham school for some months; his uncle, the Rev. W. Ayscough, rector of Burton Coggles, who had studied at Trinity, recommended Trinity College.
- brewster-0009 (biography, ch. II): admitted to Trinity 5 June 1660 (see Gaps on date), in his 18th year. Brewster says he arrived knowing less science than most students.

## Exact quotes

Newton's own words reproduced by Brewster:
- "excelled in making verses," (brewster-0005; Newton's own words to Conduitt, reported in Brewster's quote marks)

Not his words (do not put in quotes as Newton's; usable as remembered descriptions):
- "a sober, silent, thinking lad," (brewster-0003, chittenden-0002) is a description of him from Grantham witnesses, not his own phrase. He might say that others called him a sober, silent, thinking lad, but then the line is attributed to others; safest not to quote it.
- The King Charles verses (brewster-0005, "A secret art my soul requires to try, If prayers can give me what the wars deny.") were believed by Mrs. Vincent to be his; the attribution is uncertain. If used, Newton should call them lines he is thought to have written, or not claim them firmly. They appear in brewster-0005 verbatim if needed.

There are no primary-source passages on his childhood: all of this chapter is memory from the biographies.

## Gaps

- **No writings by Newton about his childhood** in our sources. Everything is biography; the kick story and the "excelled in making verses" remark are the only items traced to his own telling (via Conduitt).
- **Feelings about his mother, stepfather and grandmother:** the sources say nothing about how he felt being left with his grandmother, or about Barnabas Smith beyond his name and parish. The famous notebook confession of threatening to burn his mother and stepfather "and the house over them" is NOT in our sources. Do not invent resentment; he can say it plainly and briefly, or say he will not speak of it.
- **Grandmother's name** is not given (only "her own mother"). Nothing is said about her personality or care of him.
- **Mother's name:** Brewster calls her "Harriet Ayscough" (brewster-0001); Chittenden doesn't name her. The writer should simply say my mother rather than use a first name.
- **Father's name conflict:** Brewster's text says his father was "Mr. Isaac Newton" (brewster-0001), but the 1798 tablet quoted in brewster-0289 and chittenden-0062 says "son of John Newton". Have him say my father and avoid the name, or use Isaac per brewster-0001.
- **Date of entering Trinity:** brewster-0009 and chittenden-0003 say admitted 5 June 1660; brewster-0012 says the university books record him admitted sub-sizer in 1661. The sources disagree; avoid giving a year, or say at eighteen.
- **Sizar duties / being poor at Cambridge:** only the bare term "sub-sizer" (brewster-0012); no detail on serving other students. Don't invent.
- **Teachers who mattered at Grantham:** only Mr. Stokes is named (master; portrait drawn by Newton). No details of what he taught or how he encouraged him; nothing on what Newton studied there (Latin etc.).
- **What he hated:** the sources say farming and business "proved utterly distasteful to him" (chittenden-0003) and that he disliked "every other occupation" than study (brewster-0007). Nothing more specific.
- Brewster's uncertainties: how the mouse was driven (two versions); whether all his Woolsthorpe dials were from observation; the farm income "probable"; the love for Miss Storey "there is reason to believe"; the water clock "probably" led to dials.
- Christmas Day is Old Style (brewster-0001 gives it as Old Style). The sources do not give a New Style date; don't add one.
- "exactly one year after Galileo died" (brewster-0001) is Brewster's remark; Newton may mention it only as something he was told, and should not use later framings such as a torch being passed.
