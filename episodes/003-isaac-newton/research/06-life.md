# Money, success and happiness

Goal: Practical life wisdom from the guest's own writings: ambition, wealth, reputation, what makes a good life.
Target: 8 min (~1200 words)

Beats:
- What chasing wealth and status does to a person.
- What actually makes people happy, in the guest's view.
- Advice to a young person starting a career today.

## Questions

1. You sat on the Opticks for decades "to avoid being engaged in Disputes". Was your peace of mind worth more to you than the credit? (opticks-0000, brewster-0041, brewster-0123)
2. Yet when Leibnitz said you copied him, you fought hard. Is reputation something worth fighting for? (brewster-0171, brewster-0284)
3. For years you were a poor professor, and then Montague made you Warden of the Mint, later Master, worth twelve or fifteen hundred pounds a year. Did money change you? (brewster-0203, -0206, -0207, -0177, -0186, -0285, -0286)
4. You died rich, but your friends say you gave money away all the time. Why? (brewster-0286, chittenden-0050, brewster-0275, brewster-0004)
5. What drove you: truth, God, or fame? (principia-0783, opticks-0315, opticks-0345, brewster-0241, daniel-0179)
6. The famous line about the boy on the sea-shore with the pebbles: did you really say that, and what did you mean? (brewster-0284, chittenden-0057)
7. How did you actually work? People say you forgot to eat. (brewster-0286, chittenden-0041, brewster-0277, brewster-0118)
8. You once wrote a young man a whole set of rules for travelling abroad. What would you tell a young person starting out today? (brewster-0302 to -0306, principia-0002, opticks-0344, principia-0566)

## Passages

Disputes, delay, reputation
- opticks-0000 (primary, Advertisement I, 1704): part written 1675 for the Royal Society, rest about twelve years later; "To avoid being engaged in Disputes about these Matters, I have hitherto delayed the printing"; published only because of "the Importunity of Friends"; does not want it translated without his consent; aims to "communicate what I have tried, and leave the rest to others".
- opticks-0001 (primary, Advertisement I): he published his method of quadrature because "some Years ago I lent out a Manuscript" and later "met with some Things copied out of it". A quiet hint of priority worries; mentions his 1679 letter to Leibnitz.
- opticks-0002 (primary, Advertisement II, 1717): added Queries; frames the gravity question as a question "because I am not yet satisfied about it for want of Experiments".
- brewster-0041 (biography, ch. IV): after the 1672-75 optics disputes he resolves "to be no farther solicitous about matters of philosophy"; "so substantial a blessing as my quiet" (letter to Leibnitz, 9 Dec 1675).
- brewster-0123, -0124 (biography, ch. XI): wants to suppress Principia Book III over Hooke; "Philosophy is such an impertinently litigious lady"; Halley talks him round.
- brewster-0219, -0220 (biography, ch. XV): Abbé Conti broke a promise of secrecy and his chronology abstract was printed in Paris with a refutation; Newton's letter of 27 May 1725: "That which I wrote was not at all done with design to publish it." He then wrote a public reply.
- brewster-0283 (biography, ch. XIX): Brewster says Newton "kept back his discoveries till they were nearly perfected, and therefore withheld the successive steps of his inquiries" (biographer's judgement).
- brewster-0284 (biography, ch. XIX): Brewster: his modesty "was not founded on any indifference to the fame"; "he knew his place as a philosopher, and was determined to assert and vindicate his rights" (biographer). Then the sea-shore saying (see quotes).
- brewster-0171 (biography, ch. XII): Leibnitz affair: he resisted pressure to reply until George I asked; then wrote to Conti "with dignified severity".
- brewster-0309 (biography, Conduit memorandum): refuses to publish conjectures: "I do not deal in conjectures."

Money and the Mint
- brewster-0177 (biography, ch. XIII): around 1690 "His income was certainly very confined, and but little suited to the generosity of his disposition"; relatives made demands; "there is reason to think" he hoped for a government post (Brewster's inference).
- brewster-0186 (biography, Newton to Pepys, 1693): "I never designed to get any thing by your interest, nor by King James’s favour" (his words, written during his illness).
- brewster-0192 (biography): letters to Locke 1691-2 about a hoped-for appointment; his suspicion that "there was a design to sell him an office" (reported, then apologised for in brewster-0194).
- brewster-0203 (biography, ch. XIV): at 53 still "in comparative poverty", only the professor's salary and "the small rental of his paternal inheritance". Brewster's complaint about England neglecting genius is the biographer's opinion.
- brewster-0204, -0205 (biography): coinage "adulterated and debased"; Montague consulted Newton, Locke and Halley on the recoinage.
- brewster-0206 (biography, Montague's letter, 19 March 1695): Warden "worth five or six hundred pounds per annum, and has not too much business". Montague's words, not Newton's.
- brewster-0207 (biography): his "mathematical and chymical knowledge" useful; recoinage done "in the short space of two years"; Master 1699, "worth twelve or fifteen hundred pounds per annum", held for life; wrote an official report on the coinage and a table of assays of foreign coins; gave his Cambridge deputy Whiston "all the emoluments" and got him the chair in 1703.
- brewster-0209 (biography): Royal Society President from 1703, re-elected every year; knighted by Queen Anne at Trinity Lodge, 16 April 1705; then lost the Cambridge election "by a very great majority" (Brewster speculates why: "perhaps", "more probable").
- brewster-0285 (biography, ch. XIX): in London lived "in a very handsome style", carriage, three male and three female servants; frugal diet, simple dress, except a suit of laced clothes he "is said to have put on" for the 1705 election.
- brewster-0286 (biography, ch. XIX): "they who gave away nothing till they died never gave at all" (his remark, reported in indirect speech); "always a contempt for money" (Brewster); "enormous" sums to relations; in 1724 offered 20l. a year toward Maclaurin's salary at Edinburgh.
- chittenden-0050 (biography): same remark: "Those, he would often remark, who gave away nothing till they died, never gave at all." Still indirect speech, so the wording is Chittenden's.
- brewster-0275 (biography, ch. XVIII): personal estate "about 32,000l."; gifts of estates before death to Mrs. Conduit's relatives and to Catharine Conduit.
- brewster-0004 (biography): helped Mrs. Vincent (Miss Storey) with her "little pecuniary difficulties" all his life.

Drive: truth and God
- principia-0783 (primary, General Scholium): to discourse of God "from the appearances of things, does certainly belong to Natural Philosophy"; and yet gravity's cause is left open: "I frame no hypotheses".
- opticks-0315 (primary, Qu. 28): "every true Step made in this Philosophy brings us not immediately to the Knowledge of the first Cause, yet it brings us nearer to it, and on that account is to be highly valued."
- opticks-0345 (primary, Qu. 31 end): perfecting natural philosophy would enlarge moral philosophy; duty to God and to one another "will appear to us by the Light of Nature".
- brewster-0241 (biography, letters to Bentley): in Book III of the Principia "he had an eye upon such principles as might work, with considering men, for the belief of a Deity" (Brewster's quotation in the third person, so not verbatim Newton; do not quote).
- daniel-0179 (primary, Observations upon the Prophecies, Part II ch. I): "The folly of Interpreters has been, to foretel times and things by this Prophecy"; God gave prophecy to be understood "after they were fulfilled". A humility about prediction that fits a hot-takes or advice moment.
- brewster-0285 (biography): toleration and "abhorrence of persecution" (Brewster); rebuke to Halley (quote in 05).

Work habits and concentration
- brewster-0286 (biography): sat on his bedside for hours after rising, undressed, absorbed; had to be reminded to eat.
- chittenden-0041 (biography): kept the subject "constantly before him" and "waited till the first dawning opened gradually into a full and clear light"; could break off and resume at "the very point where they ceased". Chittenden's description.
- brewster-0277 (biography) and chittenden-0041: "he himself declared, that whatever service he had done to the public was not owing to any extraordinary sagacity, but solely to industry and patient thought". **Reported in indirect speech, no source given. Not a quote.** The guest can paraphrase it as a memory.
- brewster-0118 (biography): in 1682, redoing the Moon calculation, he grew so agitated he handed it to a friend.
- brewster-0002 (biography): schoolboy kicked in the stomach by the boy above him "laboured incessantly till he got above him". Newton told Conduit this. A good origin of his habit of application.
- opticks-0287 (primary): "I was then interrupted": he left work unfinished and handed it on as Queries.
- brewster-0175 (biography): 1669-1695 seldom away from college more than three or four weeks a year.

Advice to the young
- brewster-0302 to -0306 (biography, ch. XIX, appendix): full letter to Francis Aston, Trinity College, 18 May 1669, when Newton was 26: rules for company (observe humours, ask rather than assert, praise rather than blame, pass over affronts abroad, keep reason above passion) and things to observe (laws, trades, ships, mines, metal transmutations, prices). Ends "I am very weary". All Newton's own words; old spelling (mee, soe, discours) must be copied exactly.
- brewster-0287 (biography): Brewster cites this letter as proof Newton was not ignorant of the world.
- principia-0002 (primary, Preface): "I heartily beg that what I have here done may be read with candour".
- opticks-0344 (primary): analysis before composition; induction is the best way of arguing available.
- principia-0566 (primary, Rule IV): hold conclusions from phenomena until new phenomena correct them.

Letter to Francis Aston, 18 May 1669 (brewster-0303 to -0305), Newton's own words
- "Let your discours be more in querys and doubtings than peremptory assertions or disputings, it being the designe of travellers to learne, not to teach." (brewster-0303)
- "You will find little or no advantage in seeming wiser or much more ignorant than your company." (brewster-0303)
- "It is safer to commend any thing more than it deserves, than to discommend a thing soe much as it deserves" (brewster-0303)
- "If you bee affronted, it is better, in a forraine country, to pass it by in silence, and with a jest, though with some dishonour, than to endeavour revenge" (brewster-0303)
- "In a word, if you can keep reason above passion, that and watchfullnesse will be your best defendants." (brewster-0303)

- "It will not be time mispent to make a catalogue of the names and excellencys of those men that are most wise, learned, or esteemed in any nation." (brewster-0304)

## Gaps

- **Sea-shore / pebble saying.** It appears only in the two biographies (brewster-0284, chittenden-0057), word for word the same. It is introduced as said "a short time before his death". **Neither biographer names a source or a listener**, and it is not in any of Newton's own indexed writings. It is a recorded saying and may be quoted from those passages, but the guest should treat it as something he said late in life, in conversation, not as something he wrote.
- **"Industry and patient thought"** is only in indirect speech (brewster-0277, chittenden-0041), with no source given. Paraphrase it, do not quote it.
- **"they who gave away nothing till they died never gave at all"** is indirect speech in both biographies, worded slightly differently. Paraphrase only.
- **"Contempt for money"** is Brewster's characterisation, not Newton's words.
- **South Sea Bubble:** not in the sources (the only "South Sea" hit is geographical, in the Principia). Do not mention his losses or the famous line about the madness of people. They are not in our texts.
- **Mint prosecutions, counterfeiters, hangings:** not in the sources (confirmed: no hits for coiners or counterfeit). The sources give only the recoinage, the posts, the salaries, the coinage report and the assay table. Note: Brewster dates his appointment as Warden by Montague's letter of 19 March 1695 (probably Old Style, so March 1696 by modern reckoning). The figure file says 1696. Keep to "1695/6" or avoid the exact year.
- **Alchemy:** the only material is brewster-0254 (Law's second-hand report of furnaces, Brewster doubting he believed in alchemy) and the Aston letter's interest in "transmutations" of metals (brewster-0304/-0305). Do not build an alchemy segment.
- **Happiness:** Newton never says directly what makes a person happy. The closest material is the "quiet" he valued over fame (brewster-0041), his refusal to publish conjectures, his religious sense of duty (opticks-0345), and his generosity. The guest should reason from these, not state a philosophy of happiness.
- **"Shoulders of giants"** letter: not in the sources. Confirmed: no hit for "shoulders" or "giants".
- **Advice to a modern young person:** nothing direct. Use the Aston letter (written for a young traveller), the Preface's plea for candour, and the method passages. The guest should frame these as what he once told a young friend.
